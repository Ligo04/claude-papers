                                                                                     A BSTRACT

                                                  Neural simulation of high-fidelity deformable bodies is a foundational chal-
                                                  lenge in computer graphics and physical AI. Long-horizon prediction for high-
                                                  resolution 3D volumetric meshes is difficult: autoregressive methods are suscep-
                                                  tible to error accumulation, while direct multi-frame prediction at native resolu-
                                                  tion is computationally prohibitive. This motivates a compact spatiotemporal la-
                                                  tent representation, which remains largely unexplored for mesh-based volumetric
                                                  physics. Meanwhile, it remains unclear whether deterministic regression or gen-
                                                  erative diffusion is the more appropriate predictive paradigm. To address these
                                                  coupled challenges, we introduce PhysLDM, a unified latent-diffusion paradigm
                                                  for one-shot volumetric deformable simulation. Its core is a holistic spatiotempo-
                                                  ral VAE (ST-VAE) that avoids the “staircase” artifacts of standard temporal com-
                                                  pression (as in common video VAEs) and disentangles rigid motion from local
                                                  deformation, achieving ∼2.48 mm reconstruction precision on meter-scale scenes
                                                  at up to 78× token compression. Based on this reliable latent space, we system-
                                                  atically compare regression and diffusion paradigms. Our experiments uncover a
                                                  key modeling insight: complex deformable dynamics are often chaotic, and in this
                                                  regime deterministic regression tends to produce damped, non-physical averages,
                                                  whereas diffusion better models their distribution. Accordingly, we employ a la-
                                                  tent diffusion model that effectively learns from the chaotic data to generate phys-
                                                  ically plausible trajectories. Trained purely kinematically on an Objaverse-scale
                                                  simulation dataset, a single PhysLDM generalizes zero-shot to unseen OOD ob-
                                                  ject datasets (GSO and Toys4K). Its differentiability further enables efficient solu-
                                                  tion of inverse problems and higher-order design optimization. To our knowledge,
                                                  PhysLDM is the first high-fidelity spatiotemporal autoencoder and latent-diffusion
                                                  paradigm for volumetric deformable dynamics, offering a scalable and robust ap-
                                                  proach to neural simulation. Project Page: https://yucrazing.github.io/physldm/



                                         1   I NTRODUCTION

                                         Simulating the dynamics of deformable solids is essential to fields involving physical interaction,
                                         including computer graphics, engineering, and robotics. Furthermore, learning these complex dy-
                                         namics as predictive physical world models is also considered essential for physical AI. However,
                                         fulfilling this vision with traditional simulators remains a fundamental bottleneck: classical high-
                                         precision solvers are accurate but slow, and their gradients are hard to obtain. Neural simulation
                                         is an emerging alternative: its goal is to learn these complex physical behaviors directly from data,
                                         offering fast, differentiable surrogates that bypass the computational bottlenecks of classical solvers
                                         (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020; Pfaff et al., 2021). Nevertheless, high-fidelity deformable simu-
                                         lation demands a high-resolution geometric representation, typically a volumetric mesh with thou-
                                         sands to tens of thousands of vertices. Efficiently and accurately predicting the long-horizon trajec-
                                         tories of such large-scale systems poses significant challenges for conventional neural architectures.

Achieving this, however, presents several tightly coupled challenges. First, high-fidelity 3D vol-
umetric meshes contain large, object-dependent numbers of vertices, resulting in extremely high-
dimensional system states. Existing autoregressive (AR) approaches predict the dynamics frame
by frame, without jointly modeling the full temporal horizon, and are therefore susceptible to com-
pounding errors and trajectory drift over long rollouts. One-shot prediction of the entire trajectory
provides global temporal context, but operating directly at native resolution is computationally pro-
hibitive. Consequently, compressing these complex dynamics into a spatiotemporal latent space
becomes necessary, yet largely unexplored for mesh-based volumetric physics. This introduces the
first challenge: a representational bottleneck. In our explorations, we find that standard compression
methods (e.g., with uniform temporal strides and local temporal upsamplers, as in standard video-
VAE designs (Yu et al., 2023)) lose high-frequency temporal information and make reconstructed
shapes jump unnaturally, producing a “staircase” artifact that we analyze in Sec. 4 (Staircase analy-
sis). Furthermore, directly compressing raw world coordinates forces models to entangle large-scale
rigid motion with millimeter-scale local deformations, severely degrading precision.
The second challenge is the optimal predictive paradigm: it remains an open question whether de-
terministic regression or generative diffusion is better suited for complex physical simulation, and a
systematic comparison in the context of 3D volumetric physics is currently absent. Our systematic
experiments reveal a critical insight: microscopic non-determinism can be strongly amplified by
the inherently chaotic nature of complex deformable dynamics. For instance, state-of-the-art GPU-
based physical solvers (Huang et al., 2024a; 2025) exhibit only slight microscopic non-determinism,
yet chaos can amplify this small difference into vastly different final trajectories (Sec. 4, Chaos anal-
ysis). In this chaotic regime, nearly identical initial conditions can correspond to multiple macro-
scopically different yet physically plausible futures. Deterministic regression tends to average over
these modes, producing damped trajectories that may match none of them, whereas generative dif-
fusion can represent and sample from the multi-modal distribution of plausible outcomes.
To overcome these tightly coupled representational and predictive limitations, we propose
PhysLDM, a latent-diffusion paradigm for one-shot volumetric deformable simulation. The founda-
tion of PhysLDM is a novel holistic spatiotemporal variational autoencoder (ST-VAE) specifically


                                                   2
designed for mesh-based volumetric physics. By employing a holistic temporal compression strat-
egy and explicitly decoupling rigid body motion from local deformation, our ST-VAE eliminates
“staircase” artifacts and preserves fine geometric details. This design achieves a ∼2.48 mm average
geometric reconstruction precision for meter-scale scenes at up to a 78× token compression. By
employing a Latent Diffusion Model (LDM) (Rombach et al., 2022), PhysLDM models the distri-
bution of physically plausible future trajectories, whereas deterministic regression produces damped
averages in our experiments.
A prominent feature of our paradigm is its constitutive-model-agnostic nature. Supervised purely
kinematically on an Objaverse-scale dataset, a single PhysLDM demonstrates strong zero-shot gen-
eralization capabilities to unseen, out-of-distribution (OOD) datasets. We further demonstrate the
utility of PhysLDM as a differentiable simulator for inverse problems and design optimization.
To our knowledge, PhysLDM yields the first high-fidelity spatiotemporal autoencoder and latent-
diffusion paradigm for volumetric deformable dynamics, offering a scalable and robust approach to
neural simulation.
In summary, our main contributions include:

       • A high-fidelity ST-VAE for 3D volumetric deformable dynamics. We propose a novel
         holistic temporal compression scheme for volumetric mesh deformable dynamics, which
         removes the “staircase” artifacts that persist under standard temporal compression. By ex-
         plicitly decoupling rigid motion and deformation, we achieve ∼2.48mm average geometric
         reconstruction precision for meter-scale scenes with up to 78× token compression.
       • A systematic comparison of deterministic regression and generative diffusion
         paradigms in physical simulation. We reveal that the inherently chaotic nature of com-
         plex deformable dynamics can amplify the non-determinism that GPU solvers exhibit under
         identical initial conditions, and show that in this chaotic regime diffusion is better suited
         than deterministic regression, whose outputs collapse toward damped averages.
       • A constitutive-model-agnostic latent diffusion paradigm with strong zero-shot gener-
         alization. We present a latent-diffusion paradigm and train a single model, PhysLDM, on
         Objaverse-scale simulations (Deitke et al., 2023) supervised purely kinematically. Our
         model transfers zero-shot to unseen OOD object datasets (GSO (Downs et al., 2022),
         Toys4K (Stojanov et al., 2021)) with remarkable robustness.
       • A differentiable simulator for inverse problems and design optimization. PhysLDM
         supports end-to-end and higher-order differentiation, enabling efficient material estimation
         and second-order design optimization tasks that are difficult or computationally prohibitive
         for existing differentiable simulators.

2   R ELATED W ORK
Physics-based deformable simulation. Physics-based Lagrangian simulation has a rich history in
computer graphics, evolving from early elastic models (Terzopoulos et al., 1987) to finite element
methods (FEM) (Irving et al., 2004), contact handling (Bridson et al., 2002), and others (Müller
et al., 2007; Bouaziz et al., 2014). Recently, barrier-based methods such as incremental poten-
tial contact (IPC) provide strict intersection-free guarantees, but incur high computational costs (Li
et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2024a; 2025; Tang et al., 2026; Ando, 2024). Conversely, real-time solvers
achieve interactive rates at the expense of physical fidelity, and they cannot learn the behavior of
high-accuracy solvers (Chen et al., 2024; Giles et al., 2025). Differentiable simulators restore gra-
dients, but either require tedious hand-derived formulations or need to backpropagate through every
integration step (Huang et al., 2024b; The Newton Contributors, 2025). In contrast, PhysLDM serves
as a fast, end-to-end differentiable neural surrogate learned directly from high-accuracy solvers.
Neural simulators for deformable dynamics. Neural simulators provide a fast, fully differentiable
alternative to traditional solvers. The dominant paradigm of neural simulators is the AR rollout,
which predicts the next state frame-by-frame. This paradigm has been adopted across diverse phys-
ical simulation fields (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020; Ummenhofer et al., 2020; Pfaff et al., 2021;
Zhang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). However, AR methods lack the global temporal context
necessary for long-horizon consistency, potentially leading to temporal incoherence or trajectory
drift. The concurrent work of Chen et al. (2026) performs one-shot multi-frame prediction, but is


                                                  3
restricted to low-resolution surface meshes (training on about 356 vertices per scene) from a limited
set of templates (35 different meshes). In contrast, PhysLDM introduces a latent-diffusion paradigm
capable of predicting high-resolution volumetric trajectories in a single diffusion pass, demonstrat-
ing strong zero-shot generalization to diverse OOD scenes.
Spatiotemporal latent representations. High-fidelity volumetric simulation involves high-
resolution meshes, making direct multi-frame prediction computationally prohibitive at native
resolutions. However, spatiotemporal autoencoders remain largely unexplored for high-fidelity,
volumetric-mesh deformable-body physical simulation. In fact, existing autoencoders for physi-
cal simulation typically compress only the spatial domain (Barbic & James, 2005; Fulton et al.,
2019; Romero et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023; Chang et al., 2023; Modi et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2026;
Wiewel et al., 2019; Han et al., 2022), with concurrent neural simulators following a similar pattern
(Zhang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Geng et al., 2026). While spatiotemporal autoencoders have
been successfully deployed in other domains, their architectures are tailored to their respective tasks,
such as video generation (Yu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025) and other fields (Petrovich et al., 2021;
Cao et al., 2024). Unfortunately, these designs do not transfer directly to deformable physics. For
example, transplanting the standard temporal compression of video VAEs produces severe staircase
artifacts (as analyzed in Sec. 4, Staircase analysis). To our knowledge, our ST-VAE is the first
spatiotemporal autoencoder designed specifically for high-fidelity deformable simulation.
Prediction paradigms: regression versus diffusion. For deformable-body physical simulation,
deterministic regression and generative diffusion have never been systematically compared. In prac-
tice, most work in neural simulation uses regression (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020; Pfaff et al.,
2021; Zhang et al., 2026; Dou et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Meanwhile, diffusion (Ho et al.,
2020) has instead been adopted where output distributions matter, such as image and video synthe-
sis (Rombach et al., 2022; Ho et al., 2022) and human motion (Tevet et al., 2023). Although the
concurrent work of Chen et al. (2026) claims that diffusion is superior to deterministic regression,
motivated by the diversity of futures under unobserved initial conditions, it never compares one-shot
regression with diffusion. By contrast, we systematically compare both paradigms and find that un-
der the chaotic dynamics of complex deformable bodies diffusion is more suitable, and regression
may collapse onto non-physical averages.

3     M ETHODOLOGY
3.1   P ROBLEM F ORMULATION

We formulate the physical simulation of volumetric objects as a trajectory prediction task. Let a
scene be represented as a tetrahedral mesh with rest vertices r ∈ RN ×3 . The initial state of the
system is defined as s0 = (r, R0 , c0 , v0 , ω 0 , E), which encompasses the rest geometry, the frame-0
pose, linear velocity, and angular velocity, together with the Young’s modulus E of the material.
Our goal is to model the distribution p(X | s0 ) of future vertex trajectories X ∈ RT ×N ×3 over a
horizon of T frames. Rather than modeling the raw world coordinates directly, we factor each frame
into a global rigid transform acting on a canonical deformation:
                                       xt = Rt (r + dt ) + ct ,                                     (1)
                                  3
where Rt ∈ SO(3) and ct ∈ R denote the per-frame rigid rotation and centroid translation, and
dt represents the residual canonical (rigid-free) deformation field. Rt and ct are extracted from
each ground-truth frame in closed form by a per-frame Procrustes (Kabsch) fit of the rest shape
(Umeyama, 1991). This factoring separates the large-scale rigid motion from the small-scale de-
formation. The rigid transform (Rt , ct ) carries the global motion in six degrees of freedom per
frame. The canonical deformation field dt then only has to capture the local deformation, which is
independent of the object’s pose.

ST-VAE. The ST-VAE compresses the input T -frame trajectory by a factor of T /M into a compact
latent Z. Each frame is represented by K+1 tokens: the K deformation tokens from the spatial VAE
and one additional rigid token that embeds the pose (Rt and ct ) in its channels. This token sequence
first passes through axial blocks that alternate self-attention across tokens within a frame and along
the temporal axis (Ho et al., 2019; Bertasius et al., 2021; Arnab et al., 2021), after which it is
decoupled into a deformation track and a rigid track. For the deformation track, the standard choice
(as in video VAEs) would merge every T /M frames into one latent frame and then decode it with a
local temporal upsampler. However, this standard design introduces the staircase artifacts analyzed
in Sec. 4 (Staircase analysis). We instead employ a holistic temporal representation where time is
summarized by factorized self-attention: for each of the K spatial tokens separately, M learnable
queries are prepended to its temporal sequence, attended along the time axis, and kept as the only
temporal outputs, giving K ×M tokens in total, which we call holistic temporal tokens. For the rigid
track, sharing the holistic tokens with the deformation causes capacity competition and degrades the
reconstruction (see ablation in Sec. 4.1). The rigid token therefore takes a dedicated linear temporal
pathway and recombines with the canonical shape per frame during decoding. Ultimately, the two
tracks merge into the single joint latent Z that serves as the training target of the LDM. The ST-VAE
decoder is kept frozen during the LDM stage to decode latents back into full trajectories.
LDM. Conditioned on the initial state, the LDM predicts the latent of the full T -frame trajectory in
one pass. The frame-0 mesh is injected via cross-attention on its spatial latent, while the pose, the
initial velocity, and the stiffness condition the network via adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN)
alongside the flow timestep. The mesh topology enters only through the frozen decoder, whose
graph neural network (GNN) refines the decoded vertices over the tetrahedral mesh. We employ
a flow-matching (Lipman et al., 2023) diffusion transformer (DiT) (Peebles & Xie, 2023) utilizing
rectified flow (Liu et al., 2023) to model the probability path. Architecture details are in Appendix A.


3.3      T RAINING

Our model is trained in three stages. The first stage trains the spatial VAE with a Charbonnier
(smoothed L1 ) position-reconstruction loss and a Kullback–Leibler (KL) loss (see the spatial-VAE-
pretraining ablation in Appendix F). The second stage trains the ST-VAE on top of the pretrained
spatial VAE. Its loss consists mainly of deformation and rigid reconstruction losses over all T frames
and a KL loss, together with a latent-anchor loss to the spatial manifold. The third stage trains the


                                                                                5
LDM on the pretrained, frozen ST-VAE, with a flow-matching velocity mean-squared error (MSE)
loss. Complete loss forms, weights, and training details are given in Appendix A.
Constitutive-Model-Free Supervision. Notably, our framework relies entirely on kinematic su-
pervision. Every reconstruction loss is computed directly from geometry, namely vertex positions,
velocities, and rigid poses. No constitutive models are used during training or inference. This purely
data-driven approach makes PhysLDM agnostic to the underlying simulator’s energy model, allow-
ing it to seamlessly scale across diverse materials, and giving it the potential to learn from real-world
captured trajectories where explicit energy models are unavailable (Appendix M).

4   E XPERIMENTS

Data and setup. We generate high-accuracy physical simulation data with a GPU FEM solver
based on IPC (Huang et al., 2024a; 2025). For each scene, we randomly select n objects (n ≤ 3)
from more than 30,000 Objaverse objects, tetrahedralize them with fTetWild (Hu et al., 2020) into
volumetric meshes, and assign each object a random initial velocity (v0 ) and a random stiffness
(Young’s modulus, E). The simulation time step is 1/60 s and each trajectory has 64 frames. The
Objaverse scenes (63,397 single-object and 16,078 multi-body trajectories) are split into training,
validation, and held-out test sets at a ratio of 90% : 5% : 5%. For OOD evaluation, we generate
scenes in the same way from the objects of two other datasets, GSO and Toys4K. The ST-VAE and
the LDM are trained on scenes with N ∈ [500, 5,000] vertices. The ST-VAE and LDM results in
this paper are trained on single-object scenes (n=1). Our extended model is trained on multi-object
scenes (n ≤ 3; see the multi-body extension in Appendix J). In the controlled ST-VAE comparisons,
we use ST-VAE Small (K=512, T =32, M =4, 95M parameters). In the LDM experiments, we use
ST-VAE Base (K=512, T =64, M =8, 124M parameters; Table 3). Both configurations compress a
trajectory by 8N/513 in token count, which reaches 78× at the 5,000-vertex cap. Training details
are in Appendix A. Full data details, evaluation setup, and metric definitions are in Appendix B.

Staircase analysis. Standard temporal compression methods (such as video VAEs) uniformly
compress consecutive frames into fixed latent blocks along the time axis and decode them with
a local temporal upsampler, but this approach is not suitable for 3D volumetric deformable dynam-
ics trajectory reconstruction: it causes the object’s deformation to hold and then snap to the next
state, creating an unnatural “staircase” artifact that severely degrades visual fidelity. While this tim-
ing error is easily perceptible in video playback, it is difficult to identify from static multi-frame
snapshots alone; therefore, we quantify it via the deformation-change correlation (DCC), defined in
Appendix B.3. Although DCC does not solely measure overall reconstruction quality on its own,
it plays a decisive role in evaluating the physical plausibility of the motion when reconstruction
precision is comparable. A detailed analysis of the staircase artifact is given in Appendix C.

Chaos analysis. Although physics simulators are commonly assumed to produce deterministic
trajectories once initial states and parameters are fully specified, this is not guaranteed for GPU
solvers. As shown in Fig. 7, running the same scene five times from byte-identical inputs yields vis-
ibly different final poses, with corresponding vertices landing up to 442 mm apart. This divergence
stems from two factors: first, the underlying hardware non-determinism of GPU solvers introduces
negligible initial variations; second, the inherently chaotic nature of complex deformable dynamics
can rapidly amplify these microscopic errors into macroscopic trajectory divergence. Consequently,
training data is not a deterministic function of initial conditions. We also verify this at scale on the
300 Objaverse held-out scenes: running each scene twice from identical initial conditions, the final-
frame vertex trajectories of 23.7% of these meter-scale scenes differ by more than 1% of the object
size (about 1 cm), and those of 7.7% differ by more than 5% (about 5 cm) (Table 5). This chaotic
nature suggests why regression models struggle: faced with multiple branching futures, a squared-
error loss would drive the model toward the conditional mean, collapsing incompatible outcomes
into a damped and non-physical average. A generative approach like our LDM naturally avoids this
by sampling plausible trajectories. A more detailed chaos analysis is given in Appendix D.

Prediction Results. Table 2 and Fig. 4 compare three models predicting a 64-frame trajectory
from the same initial state: our LDM, a capacity-matched deterministic regression model (both us-
ing one-shot prediction and the same ST-VAE), and a frame-by-frame AR graph network based
on MeshGraphNets (MGN) (Appendix E.3). Because complex deformable dynamics are inherently
chaotic, world-space error alone is insufficient to evaluate prediction quality, as the ground truth rep-
resents only one of many valid outcomes. Therefore, Table 2 primarily relies on physics-consistency
checks (Appendix B; the full set of checks is in Table 8). Across all metrics and datasets, the LDM
significantly outperforms both baselines. We highlight three observations from the results. First, the
LDM consistently outperforms deterministic regression in generating physically plausible trajecto-
ries. The regression model produces damped outputs with geometric artifacts and jitter (e.g., 22.7%
volume violations versus 7.9% for the LDM on held-out scenes). In contrast, the LDM reduces these
artifacts and better preserves the deformation-to-rigid energy ratio. Second, one-shot prediction
provides the global temporal context necessary to prevent the compounding errors typically seen in
autoregressive models. Consequently, the AR baseline drifts over time and accumulates nearly three
times the world-space error of the LDM (80.6 against 28.2 mm). Third, this strong performance
transfers zero-shot to OOD datasets (GSO and Toys4K), demonstrating the robust generalization of
our approach. Finally, the LDM remains highly efficient, running at 40 to 80 frames per second on
a single GPU, even for high-resolution meshes exceeding the training cap (Appendix G).
The diffusion results in Table 2 use a single sample per scene, which cannot show whether diffusion
captures the several possible outcomes of a chaotic scene. To test whether diffusion benefits from
modeling several outcomes, we compare the two models separately on chaotic and non-chaotic
held-out scenes and draw eight diffusion samples per scene (Appendix D.6). Diffusion outperforms
regression on both subsets. One may worry that this only reflects a stronger model. However,
the diffusion samples spread much more widely on the chaotic scenes, and this spread follows the
difference between two runs of the solver. Since the model is the same on both subsets, this effect
comes from the generative paradigm and not from a stronger model. A soft key dropped onto the
ground illustrates it (Fig. 8). Run twice, the solver lets the key fall over in two different directions.


                                                            8
Every diffusion sample faithfully falls toward one of the two correct directions, while regression
predicts a non-physical average that matches neither run.


4.1   A BLATIONS

ST-VAE. We remove the ST-VAE and let a DiT of the same capacity as our LDM denoise the
vertex×frame tokens directly (Table 9). Without the latent, one-shot diffusion at native resolution is
computationally prohibitive. Memory per sample grows with the vertex count. Even on a 140 GiB
H200, the model at batch 1 runs out of memory at 2,600 vertices. With the latent, the LDM works
on a fixed number of tokens and its cost is independent of the vertex count. The vertex-dependent
cost moves to the ST-VAE stage, whose training memory grows linearly with N (Table 9), so on the
same GPU the training cap rises from about 2,500 vertices without the latent to about 14,000 for
ST-VAE Base. The latent also improves the quality of the generated geometry. A raw-coordinate
model predicts every vertex directly, so any noise left by the sampler shows up as per-vertex jitter.
Our model decodes all vertices from the latent, so the same noise only shifts the shape slightly and
coherently (illustrated in Appendix L).


The dedicated rigid pathway in ST-VAE. In the ST-VAE (Sec. 3.2), the rigid token bypasses
the holistic temporal compression and takes its own linear temporal pathway. To test this design,
we route the rigid token through the shared holistic temporal compression instead. In our exper-
iments, this one change causes two failures: the translation error explodes, and the reconstructed
deformation shrinks drastically (detailed diagnostics in Appendix F). Further ablations (spatial-VAE
pretraining and the decoder GNN) are reported in Appendix F.


4.2   D IFFERENTIABLE A PPLICATIONS

We demonstrate the end-to-end differentiability of PhysLDM on two tasks of increasing difficulty
and compare against two differentiable simulators, DiffIPC (Huang et al., 2024b) and Newton/Warp
(The Newton Contributors, 2025). The detailed comparison, together with the setup and results, is
given in Appendix K.


Material Estimation. The bottom-right block of Fig. 1 shows a material estimation task. Given a
single simulated trajectory of a body with unknown stiffness, we recover its Young’s modulus E by
gradient descent through the model, since the mismatch to the observed trajectory is differentiable in
E end to end. Because Young’s modulus is usually measured on a logarithmic scale, we optimize E
in log10 space, where an error of 1 dex means a factor of 10. To cover a wide range of stiffness, we
repeat the task on seven bodies with moduli from 3 × 104 to 3 × 106 Pa, each starting from an initial
guess up to 1.1 dex away. In all seven cases, our model recovers E within 0.06 dex in 40 Adam
steps, which takes about 40 seconds per run. DiffIPC solves the task only after its solver parameters
are tuned by hand, and then needs 26 minutes on 48 threads. Newton/Warp fails in the backward
pass, because backpropagating through its enormous number of substeps explodes the gradient.


Beyond Material Estimation: Designing the Throw. Based on the first task, we may ask a further
question: which throw reveals the stiffness best, so that the estimation above becomes easier? Easier
means that a change of E produces a larger change of the trajectory, so the trajectory carries more
information about the stiffness. We therefore maximize the sensitivity of the predicted trajectory
to E over the release velocity by gradient ascent through the model. The objective is a derivative,
so its gradient is a second-order quantity. Our model supports higher-order derivatives through the
automatic differentiation of PyTorch, so it provides this gradient directly, and 30 Adam steps finish
the design in 7 minutes. Replayed in the ground-truth simulator, the optimized throw makes the rigid
motion 13× more sensitive to the stiffness. DiffIPC offers only first-order derivatives and cannot be
applied directly to this second-order task. A workaround is to obtain the second-order gradient by
nested finite differences, but then DiffIPC is about 635× slower than our model per gradient step.
The whole design would take several days, which is not acceptable in practice. Newton/Warp does
not produce a usable first-order gradient on this scene, so a second-order one is out of reach.

5   C ONCLUSION

We introduce PhysLDM, a latent diffusion paradigm for predicting the dynamics of high-fidelity
volumetric deformable bodies. To overcome the computational limits of processing high-resolution
meshes, we developed a novel holistic ST-VAE that explicitly disentangles global rigid motion from
local deformations. This architecture eliminates the staircase artifacts inherent to standard temporal
compression, ensuring highly accurate trajectory reconstruction even for high-resolution, out-of-
distribution meshes. Operating on this compact latent representation, we demonstrate that while
deterministic regression produces damped, non-physical averages in this chaotic regime, our gener-
ative approach predicts physically consistent trajectories in a single diffusion pass. Finally, trained
purely kinematically, PhysLDM delivers robust zero-shot generalization and provides end-to-end
differentiability, unlocking complex inverse design optimizations that remain intractable for tradi-
tional solvers.
Limitations and Future Work. Currently, our model focuses on predicting 64-frame trajectories in
a single diffusion pass. Extending this one-shot approach into an autoregressive framework presents
a promising direction for future research.

A     A RCHITECTURE AND T RAINING D ETAILS

Our model is trained in three stages, one per component: the spatial VAE for 183k iterations, ST-
VAE Base for 150k iterations, and the LDM for 278k iterations. The three stages together take about
7 days on 8 H200 GPUs.

A.1   S TAGE 1: S PATIAL VAE

The spatial VAE is an 8-layer transformer of hidden width 512 (44M parameters). Its input is one
frame in canonical coordinates, i.e. the rest shape plus the deformation dt of Eq. (1) with the rigid
motion removed. The encoder cross-attends K=512 learned query tokens against the N vertex
tokens of the frame and outputs K latent tokens of width D=48, independent of N . We denote
the latent of frame t by zt . The decoder mirrors the encoder: the N rest vertices, embedded with
their positions, cross-attend the latent tokens and are decoded back to their N displacements. The
decoder additionally takes the mesh topology as input, because a shallow GNN over the tetrahedral
mesh then refines the decoded displacements. The spatial VAE is trained first with a Charbonnier
position loss and a KL term.

A.2   S TAGE 2: ST-VAE

The per-frame latents {zt }Tt=1 are processed by a stack of factorized space–time attention blocks
(Ho et al., 2019; Bertasius et al., 2021; Arnab et al., 2021). The holistic temporal compression
then summarizes the time axis: M learned query tokens attend over each spatial token’s T -frame
sequence, and only these M outputs are kept as the temporal representation, while the T per-frame
outputs are discarded. The rigid-motion token bypasses this attention compression and is com-
pressed by a separate linear temporal path. The two are kept apart because the rigid signal is orders
of magnitude larger than the deformation and, when the two share one compression path, takes over
the shared latent and starves the deformation track (Sec. 4.1, Appendix F). The two tracks then
merge: the joint latent is Z ∈ RM ×(K+1)×D of Sec. 3.2, i.e. M temporal tokens for each of the
K deformation tokens and the rigid token, each of width D=48. The decoder mirrors the encoder:
per-frame queries attend over the M temporal tokens to recover a latent for every frame, which the
spatial VAE decoder turns into vertex positions. Table 3 lists the two ST-VAE configurations used
in this paper, ST-VAE Small for the controlled comparisons of Table 1 and ST-VAE Base for the
LDM experiments (Sec. 4). Both configurations compress a trajectory by the same factor, counted
in tokens. The input has one token per vertex per frame, T × N tokens in total, and the latent has
M × (K+1) tokens. Since T /M =8 and K+1=513 in both configurations, the token compression
ratio is 8N/513, which reaches 78× at the 5,000-vertex cap.

A.3   S TAGE 3: LDM

The LDM and the regression model receive the same conditioning: the frame-0 spatial latent through
cross-attention, and the initial pose, the initial velocity v0 , and the Young’s modulus E through
adaptive layer normalization. The LDM is a DiT with 12 blocks of width 768 (170M parameters)
operating on the 4,104 latent tokens of ST-VAE Base (M × (K+1) = 8 × 513 tokens of width
D=48). It is trained with rectified flow and sampled by Euler integration. The regression model is
a capacity-matched transformer that emits the full latent trajectory in one pass. Training the spatial
VAE, ST-VAE Base, and the LDM takes about 7 days in total on 8 H200 GPUs.

B     DATA , E VALUATION S ETUP, AND M ETRICS
B.1   DATA DETAILS

All simulation data are generated with StiffGIPC (Huang et al., 2025), a GPU IPC solver. IPC
solvers are widely adopted in scenarios that demand high simulation accuracy, such as embodied AI
(Kim et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025). Such a solver runs offline. Learning its behavior and turning it into
a fast, fully differentiable neural simulator is therefore of practical importance. All 3D meshes used
for training come from the Objaverse dataset (Deitke et al., 2023). Each asset is voxel-remeshed to
a watertight surface, tetrahedralized with fTetWild (Hu et al., 2020), reduced to its largest connected
component, and rescaled to a unit bounding box. Meshes with more than 10,000 vertices are dis-
carded, and the ST-VAE and the LDM are trained on scenes with 500–5,000 vertices. Every scene
drops one meter-scale solid onto a ground plane. The object is released with its lowest point 0.85 to
1.65 m above the ground. Each object receives a random initial velocity v0 and a random stiffness
E (a horizontal v0 ∼ U (0, 2) m/s and a Young’s modulus E log-uniform in [3 × 104 , 3 × 106 ] Pa)
under a Stable Neo-Hookean material. Multi-body scenes follow the same setting with two or three
objects, each with its own E and v0 , thrown toward each other so that they collide in mid-air before
reaching the ground. In total, the dataset contains 63,397 single-object and 16,078 multi-body sim-
ulated trajectories. The simulation time step is 1/60 s, and each trajectory has 64 frames. Held-out
scenes are excluded from all three training stages, and every reported number is computed on these
held-out scenes or on the OOD sets. For OOD evaluation we generate 1,015 GSO and 3,814 Toys4K
scenes under the identical simulation setting. They never appear in training.

B.2   E VALUATION SETUP

(a) ST-VAE reconstruction (Table 1). For each evaluation set (held-out, GSO, Toys4K), we randomly
draw 300 scenes with 500 ≤ N ≤ 5,000 vertices for the main columns and 300 scenes with
N > 5,000 vertices for the large-mesh columns. Every model is evaluated on the same scenes.
(b) Prediction (Table 2). The same 300 scenes per set within 500 ≤ N ≤ 5,000. The LDM uses
50 Euler steps. Every metric is a per-scene value, reported as the median across scenes to limit the
influence of a few extreme values.

require knowledge of the underlying constitutive model. The violation rates are defined as follows.
A physical quantity (speed or acceleration) is computed for every vertex at every frame, once from
the prediction and once from the ground truth. Each such vertex-frame pair is one sample. A sample
is counted as a violation if the predicted value is more than twice, or less than half, the ground-truth
value. The violation rate is the fraction of violated samples. Samples whose ground-truth value is
too small to be meaningful are excluded by a magnitude threshold.

         one where the prediction changes the volume by more than 10%. The rate is the fraction of
         violations.

C     S TAIRCASE A NALYSIS D ETAILS
This section diagnoses the staircase artifact. It appears in four baselines of Table 7: the standard
baseline, its two variants (velocity loss and spectral loss), and adaptive compression. The architec-
tures of these baselines are described in Appendix E.1.

C.1   S YMPTOM AND DIAGNOSIS

The symptom is wrong timing of rapid motion. Where the ground truth changes quickly, for instance
at impact, the reconstruction holds its shape for several frames and then jumps to catch up, so the
motion arrives late and all at once. We diagnose it in three ways. Fig. 5 plots the deformation-change
curve on five scenes. The curves of the standard baseline, its two variants, and adaptive compression
stay below ground truth for several frames and then overshoot it in one frame. The holistic ST-VAE
follows the ground truth, and so does the direct world-coordinate baseline, which uses the same
holistic temporal compression. Fig. 6 shows the same behavior directly on the silhouettes of one
scene. The silhouettes of the standard baseline and its variants change less than the ground truth for
three frames and then more than it in the last one or two frames. Adaptive compression changes
less than the ground truth over all five frame pairs and releases the motion even later. The direct
world-coordinate baseline follows the ground truth until the last pair. Table 4 scores the five scenes
with DCC. The holistic ST-VAE is the best or tied on every scene.

C.2   C AUSE

In the standard baseline, the decoded frames at the latent-frame positions are accurate, and the
frames between them carry the error. The most likely cause is that standard compression summarizes
every k frames into one latent frame and loses the high-frequency temporal information that says
when, within those k frames, the rapid change happens. One might think that the problem is a
mismatch between neighboring blocks. This is not the case. Two observations show that the error
builds up inside each block. First, the standard baseline moves too little during the early frames
of every block and then catches up in the last frames, whereas the ground truth moves at a steady
pace throughout the block. Second, smoothing the reconstruction across block boundaries does not
remove the artifact.

C.3   W HY THE VARIANTS AND ADAPTIVE COMPRESSION DO NOT HELP

The two variants of the standard baseline and the adaptive-compression baseline all keep the ap-
proach of standard temporal compression: they compress the trajectory into a fixed number of latent
frames and then reconstruct the frames by local temporal upsampling, so they leave the artifact in
place. The velocity loss and the spectral loss change the training signal only. The standard baseline
still cannot place the rapid change at the right instant between two latent frames. Adaptive compres-
sion moves the latent frames toward the impact, which makes the steps fewer but steeper. A likely
reason is that the rebound and settling phases are left with one or two latent frames, so their motion is
released in a late snap. The direct world-coordinate baseline uses the holistic temporal compression
and shows almost no staircase. Its high DCC in Table 4 together with its lower precision in Table 1
shows that its weakness is precision rather than timing.

D     C HAOS IN T RADITIONAL GPU S IMULATORS

This section gives the details of the chaos experiment in Sec. 4 (Chaos analysis). A common as-
sumption is that a physics simulator produces a deterministic trajectory once the initial state and
parameters are fully specified. In practice, this is not guaranteed for modern GPU solvers. Because
of their speed, GPU solvers are increasingly used to generate physical simulation data at scale, so
the consequences of this non-determinism for learning deserve a closer look. Two separate facts
are involved. First, a GPU solver is not bit-reproducible: its parallel reductions add floating-point
numbers in a different order on every run, which perturbs the state at the level of rounding error. On
its own this would be harmless. Second, the complex dynamics of deformable bodies are chaotic:
it can amplify such a perturbation into a macroscopic difference in the outcome. The experiment
below separates the two.

D.1    S ETUP

We take one held-out scene, a soft tennis racket dropped onto the ground, and run the GPU solver
five times on the same GPU from byte-identical inputs: the same tetrahedral mesh, initial state,
material parameters, and solver configuration. To measure how far two runs drift apart at a given
frame, we compute the displacement of every vertex between the two runs and report the largest one
as a fraction of the object’s extent.

D.2    T HE PERTURBATION IS TINY

The non-determinism of the solver is real but minute. After the first simulation step the five runs
differ by at most 0.02 mm, two hundred-thousandths of the object size. Before the racket reaches
the ground, the runs still differ by at most 3% of the object size, mostly in the strings of the net,
and their centres of mass by less than 1 mm. Until the racket reaches the ground, the five runs are
therefore practically identical.

D.3    A MPLIFICATION AFTER IMPACT

The behavior changes qualitatively at impact. After the racket hits the ground, the runs diverge
rapidly and settle into visibly different poses (Fig. 7). Over the ten pairs of runs, the final divergence
ranges from 10% to 51% of the object size, with a median of 21%. The same vertex can land up to
442 mm apart in two runs.
To check that this is chaos and not a peculiarity of the reduction order, we replace the rounding
noise by a controlled perturbation: a deterministic initial rotation of the racket between 10−8 and
10−2 rad. The final divergence is of the same order for every magnitude and does not grow with it.
A perturbation a million times larger does not produce a larger difference. This is the signature of
chaotic dynamics. The racket has several possible resting poses. Even the smallest perturbation is
already enough to change which one it ends in, so making the perturbation larger does not make the
difference larger. It only changes which resting pose the racket lands in.

D.4    W HERE THE CHAOS COMES FROM

The sensitivity comes from complex dynamics involving contact. When the racket hits the ground,
the solver makes a discrete decision about which edge or corner touches first, and a microscopic
perturbation can flip this decision. From then on the bounce and the final pose follow different
paths. In our experiments, soft objects that are thin, branchy, or non-convex are more prone to
amplifying this chaotic effect.

Diffusion outperforms regression on both subsets. Table 6 shows that a single diffusion sample
already improves on the regression model in every metric, on both subsets. It has a lower error
than the regression model in 79% of the non-chaotic scenes and 65% of the chaotic scenes. The
advantage of diffusion is therefore not limited to chaotic scenes.
Multiple samples matter more under chaos. What differs between the two subsets is how much
the eight samples add over a single one. Picking the best of the eight lowers the median error by
3.4 mm on the non-chaotic scenes and by 9.3 mm on the chaotic scenes. The samples also spread
further apart under chaos: the gap between the best and the worst sample of a scene is 7 mm on the
non-chaotic scenes and 25 mm on the chaotic scenes (medians over scenes). On the chaotic scenes,
this spread follows the physics. It grows with the distance between the two solver runs (Spearman
ρ = 0.38, p = 0.001), and the second solver run lies inside the spread of the eight samples in 99%
of the scenes. The benefit does not depend on picking the best sample: averaged over the eight
samples, the median final-frame error on the chaotic scenes is 138 mm, below the 162 mm of the
regression model. The diffusion model is thus more uncertain where the physics is less predictable,
and this uncertainty covers outcomes that the solver itself produces.
An example: a falling soft key. In a scene with several possible outcomes, diffusion can learn
these outcomes, whereas regression can only learn a non-physical average that matches none of
them. Fig. 8 shows such a scene, a soft key. When the solver is run twice, the key falls over in two
different directions. The regression model predicts a single pose that lies between the two runs and
resembles neither. The eight diffusion samples instead fall on both sides, and the closest of them
come close to each of the two runs. Other scenes behave similarly. Among the six scenes whose
two runs end more than 30◦ apart, at least one diffusion sample ends within 30◦ of a solver run in
five scenes, while the regression model does so in only one.
An advantage of the paradigm, not of a stronger model. One may worry that the diffusion model
is simply the stronger model (the LDM uses 50 sampling steps, the regression model a single pass),
and that the results above reflect this difference. Three observations show that on chaotic scenes
the advantage comes from the paradigm. First, the diffusion samples differ from each other in a
way that follows the physics: their spread grows with the distance between the two solver runs and
contains the second run in 99% of the chaotic scenes. A deterministic model, however strong, gives
a single prediction per initial state and has no such spread. Second, where the solver itself produces
clearly different outcomes, the diffusion samples reach them, while the regression prediction is a
non-physical average between them, as in the key example. Third, the benefit of drawing several
samples depends on the scenes, not on the model. With the same model on both subsets, it is almost


                                                   26
three times larger on the chaotic scenes (9.3 against 3.4 mm), whereas a difference in model strength
would affect both subsets alike.
In summary, diffusion predicts more accurately than regression on both chaotic and non-chaotic
scenes. On chaotic scenes it also produces several plausible outcomes, while regression gives a
single non-physical average result. These results show that diffusion is better suited to chaotic
deformable dynamics.

F     M ORE A BLATIONS

This section collects the ablations that support design choices of the model: the use of the ST-VAE,
the dedicated rigid pathway, the spatial-VAE pretraining, and the decoder GNN.

F.1   T HE ST-VAE

This section gives the measurements behind the ST-VAE ablation of Sec. 4.1, which asks what hap-
pens to the training cost when the ST-VAE is removed and the LDM denoises the raw vertex×frame
tokens directly. We compare two options on the same GPU at increasing vertex counts N . The first
is our LDM architecture applied to the raw tokens, without any latent. The second is our pipeline
with the ST-VAE, where the LDM works on a fixed set of 4,104 latent tokens and the ST-VAE is the
stage with the largest memory footprint. Table 9 lists the peak training memory of both.
Without the ST-VAE the number of tokens is T ×N , so memory grows with N . The raw-token model
fits at batch 1 up to 2,500 vertices and runs out of memory at 2,600, below the median resolution of
our dataset (∼3,400 vertices). More than half of the training scenes therefore cannot be processed
at all. With the ST-VAE, the LDM costs about 4.4 GiB per trajectory at any N . The ST-VAE stage
grows linearly with N , by about 8 GiB per 1,000 vertices, and ST-VAE Base trains up to about
14,000 vertices on the same card. The latent therefore does not remove the vertex-dependent cost,
but moves it to a stage that scales far more gently.

F.2   T HE RIGID PATHWAY

Sec. 4.1 shows that routing the rigid token through the shared holistic temporal compression makes
the translation error explode and starves the deformation track: on a small held-out diagnostic set,
the translation error grows from 1.8 to 887 mm, and the reconstructed deformation shrinks to 28%
of its true magnitude (97% with the dedicated pathway). In addition, the failure is not a matter of
numerical scale. Normalizing the rigid signal to unit variance before the shared compression barely
helps (52.8 → 49.6 mm world error), whereas the dedicated linear pathway reaches 8.3 mm. The
rigid and the deformation signals compete for the capacity of the shared latent, and the dedicated
pathway removes this competition.

F.3   S PATIAL VAE PRETRAINING

This section explains why the spatial VAE needs to be pretrained first rather than trained directly
together with the ST-VAE. We compare two training schemes at a matched budget. The first pre-
trains the spatial VAE and then trains the ST-VAE on top of it with an additional latent-anchor loss
(Sec. 3.3), which ties the per-frame latents reconstructed by the ST-VAE to those of the pretrained


                                                30
spatial VAE. The second trains both stages jointly from scratch, with a randomly initialized spatial
VAE.
We find that joint training works poorly. At the standard learning rate, training diverges within a
few thousand steps. At a reduced learning rate, it converges to a much worse model: the geometric
reconstruction error is about 5× that of the pretrained scheme, the deformation is much weaker than
the ground truth, and volume is lost. The likely reason is that the two stages chase each other. The
latent that the ST-VAE learns to reproduce keeps moving while the spatial VAE is still changing, so
the temporal model has no fixed target. Pretraining fixes the target first. Once pretrained, the spatial
VAE can either be kept frozen during ST-VAE training or fine-tuned with a smaller learning rate,
and the two options give similar results. We keep it frozen for ST-VAE Small and fine-tune it for
ST-VAE Base.

F.4   T HE SPATIAL DECODER GNN

We use a GNN component in the spatial VAE decoder because the field decoder places every vertex
independently, so nothing ties neighboring vertices together, and the GNN over the tetrahedral mesh
adds this coupling. We compare three placements of the GNN: in the decoder, as in our model;
removed; and moved to the encoder instead. Without the GNN, reconstruction degrades and many
mesh edges end up stretched far from their rest length. With the GNN in the encoder, the result
is worse on every metric, because smoothing the mesh before encoding removes detail before it
reaches the latent, whereas smoothing after decoding only regularizes the output.

G     I NFERENCE RUNTIME
This section reports the inference speed of PhysLDM and how it depends on the mesh resolution. We
measure batch-1 inference on a single H200 for 64-frame trajectories on five held-out scenes with
980 to 9,851 vertices, the last three beyond the 5,000-vertex training cap. One inference pass consists
of three steps: the spatial VAE encodes frame 0, the latent model predicts the latent trajectory (one
forward pass for the regression model, 50 or 20 Euler steps for the LDM), and the ST-VAE decodes
all 64 frames. Timings are medians over repeated runs with the GPU synchronized and no I/O in the
timed region, in bf16 and in fp32. Table 10 lists the decoding time, the resulting frame rates, and
the peak GPU memory.
Two observations follow. First, the cost of the latent model is independent of the mesh resolution,
because it always works on the same 4,104 latent tokens, so its latency is the same at every N
(bf16: 9.8 ms for the regression model, 460 ms for the LDM at 20 steps, and 1,150 ms at 50). The
decoder is the only stage whose cost grows with N , and in bf16 it is nearly flat, from 318 ms at
980 vertices to 384 ms at 9,851, with no loss of accuracy. As a result, the LDM runs at about 40
to 80 frames per second in bf16 and the regression model at up to 195, even on meshes twice the
training cap. Second, the whole pass needs only about 5 GiB of GPU memory across the range.

I          E XTREME C ASES A NALYSIS


This section analyzes extreme cases. The accuracy of the LDM is fundamentally bounded by the
reconstruction performance of the ST-VAE, meaning that the most challenging cases for the ST-
VAE set the limits of the entire pipeline. Across the held-out scenes, the largest reconstruction
errors mostly occur on geometries with many long, thin features.
Fig. 12 illustrates a representative extreme case, a soft birdcage comprising 9,021 nodes, which
is 1.8 times the 5,000-vertex training cap and contains dozens of thin bars only a few tetrahedra
thick. Its mean reconstruction error over the trajectory is 9.9 mm, about twice the held-out mean
for meshes above the training cap (Table 1). This error is localized to the thin bars, which exhibit
distorted and tangled reconstructions. Nevertheless, the overall shape of the collapsed cage, its pose,
and its motion over the trajectory are all recovered.
Furthermore, node count alone is not the primary performance bottleneck. For instance, the teaser
mug shown in Fig. 1 represents a compact solid with 11,621 nodes (2.3 times the training cap) yet
achieves a lower reconstruction error of 5.3 mm. What distinguishes the birdcage is the density of
thin features that deform independently of each other. The folding of every bar is a high-frequency
deformation, and the fixed-size latent has to carry it for dozens of bars at once. The latent has enough
capacity for the global shape, but not for the fine detail of every bar.

J       M ULTI -B ODY E XTENSION
This section describes the multi-body extension of our method and presents its qualitative results.
Our approach generalizes effectively to multi-object scenes. The factorization defined in Eq. (1) is
applied at the scene level: a single global rigid transform (Rt , ct ) is fitted across the vertices of
all bodies, while the relative motion among bodies is captured by the canonical deformation field
dt . Because the spatial VAE encodes all vertices within a scene through a shared set of K query
tokens, multi-body scenes are encoded, compressed, and predicted in the same manner as single-
body objects, requiring no architectural modifications to the ST-VAE or the LDM.
We train both the ST-VAE and the LDM on scenes containing up to three bodies (n ≤ 3), where
randomly selected objects are thrown toward each other to collide in mid-air before hitting the
ground. Each body is assigned an independent random Young’s modulus and initial velocity.
Fig. 13 illustrates the ST-VAE reconstruction on four multi-body scenes across four representative
frames: immediately before and after the mid-air collision, the first frame following ground im-
pact, and the final settled state. All bodies within each scene, including their collisions and ground
contacts, are successfully reconstructed through the single global transform. Fig. 14 demonstrates
one-shot prediction on four multi-body scenes conditioned on the initial state, with predictions ren-
dered using the original textured assets. The entire scene, encompassing each individual body along
with its collision dynamics and landing behavior, is predicted end-to-end in a single diffusion pass.

K.2     B EYOND MATERIAL ESTIMATION : DESIGNING THE THROW

Building on the first task, we ask a further question: which throw makes the stiffness easiest to
estimate? A throw is informative when a small change in E produces a large change in the resulting
trajectory.

Task Setup. Crucially, how large this trajectory change is depends on the stiffness of the body
itself. Therefore, the trajectory sensitivity must be evaluated at a specific value of E, which we set
to E ⋆ (6·104 Pa). The design is thus local: it finds the most informative throw for a body of roughly
this stiffness, assuming a rough estimate of the stiffness is already available. To ensure robustness
against launch errors, we maximize the sensitivity of the predicted rigid motion to log10 E, averaged
over eight small perturbations of the release velocity v0 . Because the objective itself is a derivative,
maximizing it via gradient ascent fundamentally requires a second-order gradient.

Optimization Results. Our model supports higher-order derivatives and provides this second-
order gradient directly via reverse-mode automatic differentiation. In just 30 Adam steps (7 min-
utes), our model successfully optimizes the throw. Replaying this designed throw in the ground-truth
solver confirms its effectiveness: the sensitivity of the rigid motion to E increases by 13×, and per-
vertex sensitivity by 1.85×. This highly informative trajectory significantly widens the basin of
attraction, enabling successful E recovery even from a distant initial guess (E0 = 106 ) where the
original throw stalls. Figure 16 visualizes the ground-truth trajectories of the initial and the op-
timized throw, the sensitivity gain, and the subsequent material-estimation runs from this distant
initial guess.

Comparison with Differentiable Simulators. If the first-order task is already this challenging for
existing solvers, this second-order throw design becomes infeasible for them. DiffIPC is inherently
limited to first-order analytic adjoints; forcing it to approximate the necessary second-order gradient
requires nested finite differences (64 forward solves per step). As a result, a single gradient step takes
2.4 hours, about 635× slower than our model. The whole design would therefore take about three

M                  T RAINING ACROSS E NERGY M ODELS AND ON R EAL C APTURED
                   T RAJECTORIES
PhysLDM is trained with kinematic supervision only (Sec. 3.3). No energy model or material law
enters the data format, the losses, or the architecture. This design gives PhysLDM three practical
advantages, which we test in this section.

                   1. Diverse energy models. Because the constitutive model never enters training, the same
                      pipeline can learn from simulations with any energy model.
                   2. Data without an explicit energy model. For the same reason, PhysLDM can learn from
                      trajectories with only an implicit energy model, such as trajectories captured in the real
                      world.
                   3. Seamless support for other data formats, such as surface meshes and point clouds.
                      Our training data are tetrahedral meshes, but PhysLDM does not depend on the tetrahedra
                      themselves. The spatial VAE only takes vertex positions and a graph over the vertices. For
                      a tetrahedral mesh, the graph is given by the tetrahedral edges. A surface mesh fits this


                                                             42
         structure naturally, with its triangle edges as the graph. For a point cloud, we can use a
         k-nearest-neighbor graph or no edges at all. PhysLDM therefore runs on these formats
         without any change to the model or the training code. Because our input does not need to
         be a tetrahedral mesh, PhysLDM also supports object types beyond volumetric solids, such
         as cloth and rope, which are usually not represented with tetrahedra.

We test these advantages in two steps. First, we apply the pretrained PhysLDM, trained only on
Stable Neo-Hookean simulations of Objaverse meshes, to new data without any adaptation. Second,
we fine-tune it on the new data with the unchanged training code.


M.1    DATA

Diverse energy models. We take 40 held-out Objaverse scenes and simulate each scene twice
with StiffGIPC from the same initial state. The first run uses the Stable Neo-Hookean energy of
the training data (Smith et al., 2018). The second run uses an as-rigid-as-possible (ARAP) energy
(Sorkine & Alexa, 2007) with the same shear stiffness and no volume term, so the objects are softer
and more compressible. The two energies move the trajectories 28 mm apart (median over scenes).
We exclude the chaotic scenes of Appendix D, so this difference comes from the energy model and
not from solver noise.
Real captured point clouds. We use 22 real captured sequences from Jiang et al. (2025): 9 soft
volumetric objects (plush toys and a package), 8 pieces of cloth, and 5 ropes, each lifted, pushed, or
stretched by a human hand on a table. The data are 3D points tracked over time with three RGB-D
cameras, with 616 to 7,826 points per object. There is no mesh and no explicit energy model, and
the forces of the human hand are not observed. The setting of these scenes is also very different from
that of our training data. In our simulations, every object is released in the air with an initial velocity
and falls onto the ground. In the captured sequences, the object rests on a table and is manipulated
by a human hand. The real data are therefore out of domain for PhysLDM. We store the tracked
points as the vertices and a k-nearest-neighbor graph (k = 8) as the topology, in the same file format
as our simulated data. PhysLDM is thus seamlessly compatible with these data, without any change
to our code.
Evaluation. We use the metrics of Table 2 (Appendix B.4): the volume violation rate, the
deformation-to-rigid kinetic energy ratio, and the world-coordinate error, each reported as the me-
dian over scenes. The first two need tetrahedra and are not defined on the point clouds.


M.2    Z ERO - SHOT RESULTS

Table 16 applies the pretrained PhysLDM to the new data without any adaptation.
The ST-VAE generalizes zero-shot across energy models and to real point clouds. On the simu-
lated scenes, the reconstruction error is 4.5 mm with Stable Neo-Hookean and 4.6 mm with ARAP.
On the real point clouds it is 6.0 mm. This is the most demanding test: the scenes show manipula-
tion by a human hand instead of objects thrown onto the ground, the data are real captures instead of
simulations, the input is a point cloud instead of a tetrahedral mesh, and cloth and rope never appear
in training. Even on this out-of-domain data, the ST-VAE reconstructs the motion accurately without
any adaptation. Although the ST-VAE was trained only on Stable Neo-Hookean simulations, it rep-
resents motion under an unseen energy model (ARAP) and real motion without an explicit energy
model almost as accurately (Fig. 18). This shows that its latent space is not tied to the energy model
of the training data.
The LDM prediction depends on the training data. On the simulated scenes with Stable Neo-
Hookean, the prediction error is 23.9 mm, in line with Table 2. With ARAP, the error grows to
37.5 mm and the kinetic energy ratio drops to 0.79: the LDM predicts the deformation of the material
it was trained on, while the ARAP objects deform more. On the real sequences, the zero-shot
prediction fails (875 mm) because of the domain gap, not the energy model. The LDM has only
learned objects thrown onto the ground, while the real object stays on the table until the human hand
moves it.

M.3    F INE - TUNING WITH THE UNCHANGED PIPELINE

To adapt PhysLDM to new data, we only replace the training data with the new trajectories and
fine-tune the LDM from the pretrained weights with the same trainer. The ST-VAE stays frozen.
The same procedure applies to both energy models and to the real point clouds. Table 17 reports the
results on the fine-tuning subsets, so the values before fine-tuning differ slightly from Table 16. For
both explicit energy models, the prediction error drops by about two thirds, to 7.6 mm and 8.8 mm.
For ARAP, the kinetic energy ratio moves from 0.80 to 0.92, so the LDM learns the softer material
from the trajectories alone. On the real point clouds, the error drops from 921 to 6.2 mm: the LDM
learns from the tracked points that a resting object stays on the table and how the points move
(Fig. 18).
In summary, the same PhysLDM, with the same code and losses, handles simulations with different
energy models, real captured trajectories without an explicit energy model, and other data formats
such as point clouds. Its ST-VAE transfers to all of them zero-shot, and its LDM adapts to them by
fine-tuning. Visual results of PhysLDM on the real captured data are shown in Fig. 18.
