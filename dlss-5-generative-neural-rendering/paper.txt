Abstract:
We present DLSS 5, a real-time generative rendering stage for interactive graphics that comple-
ments conventional rendering with appearance priors learned from real-world visual data. Prior
DLSS technologies use learned reconstruction to eﬃciently approximate outputs that would other-
wise require a higher rendering budget. This improves rendering eﬃciency and image quality, but
realism remains bounded by what the renderer can represent and simulate. In practice, real-time
compute and memory budgets limit both authored scene representations and rendering algorithms,
preventing them from fully capturing real-world appearance. Foundational generative models of-
fer a complementary source of realism. They learn broad knowledge of real-world appearance and
can synthesize complex visual eﬀects that are diﬃcult to represent faithfully with authored assets
and real-time simulation, such as subsurface scattering in skin and light scattering through foliage.
However, directly applying these models to interactive rendering presents two fundamental chal-
lenges. First, general-purpose generative models do not reliably preserve developer-authored scene
content and structure, even when their outputs appear visually plausible. Second, their inference
cost exceeds the strict frame budgets of interactive rendering, particularly at 4K output resolution.
DLSS 5 is designed to harness learned appearance priors while faithfully preserving developer-
authored content and design intent. To achieve this within an interactive frame budget, we in-
troduce 3D-guided neural rendering, a renderer-grounded generative approach. The system uses a
one-step pixel-space diﬀusion model designed for high-resolution, real-time rendering. At inference,
the model is conditioned on the current rendered frame, engine motion vectors, carried temporal
state, and artistic-direction values. During training, consistency supervision from renderer-derived
scene attributes further grounds generation to remain faithful to the authored scene. The result-

DLSS 5 is the ﬁrst DLSS technology to generate the ﬁnal displayed appearance rather than recon-

them by supplying learned real-world appearance knowledge that additional samples and explicitly
authored scene representations alone cannot eﬃciently provide.


1. Introduction
Real-time rendering builds every image from an explicit description of the scene. The application
supplies geometry, materials, lights, simulation state, and cameras; the renderer turns that state
into a frame with a prescribed algorithm. Because every pixel follows from authored state, the
result is consistent, controllable, and fast enough to respond to the player.
Foundational visual generative models learn a diﬀerent route to realistic images. Trained on pho-
tographic data, they capture how the real world looks — how skin, hair, foliage, and everyday
materials appear under real light — as statistical properties of photographs rather than as physical
models (Karras et al., 2022, 2024; Esser et al., 2024; Imagen 3 Team, 2024). DLSS 5 combines
the two: it conditions a real-time generative model on the renderer’s own output, so that learned
appearance is applied to the scene the developers authored.
This introduction motivates that combination (Sec. 1.1), then states what DLSS 5 is, the principle
it is built on, and the four requirements that structure the rest of the report (Sec. 1.2), and closes
with a simpliﬁed conceptual formulation of the generation problem (Sec. 1.3).

1.1. Motivation

The cost of ﬁrst-principles realism. Real-time image quality has advanced through programmable
shading, general-purpose GPU computing, ray tracing, and neural reconstruction, and each step
has widened what ﬁts inside an interactive budget. But the closer rendering gets to the real world,
the more every remaining step costs: more computation, more memory bandwidth, and more artist
time for materials, assets, and per-scene lighting. To sustain 60 fps, the application has a budget
of roughly 16.7 ms per frame, and most of it is already spent on simulation, animation, and base
rendering. Photorealism is, in practice, limited less by knowledge of how to achieve it than by
the scale of computation, memory, and asset production required — some light transport, such
as scattering within skin, is impractical to simulate at any budget, while most content is simply
authored below achievable ﬁdelity to ﬁt platform budgets. Section 5.2 develops this argument and
the complementary, learned-appearance axis DLSS 5 adds.

Reconstruction is bounded by its target. Every prior DLSS technology reconstructs. Super
Resolution, Frame Generation, and Ray Reconstruction each estimate what the renderer would
have produced with more pixels, more frames, or more rays (NVIDIA Corporation, 2025b), and
they deliver that estimate far more cheaply than rendering it directly. The renderer deﬁnes their
target, and that target is also their ceiling: a reconstruction cannot look better than the output it
reconstructs.

Appearance learned from data. The knowledge that rendering budgets cannot provide can
instead be learned from photographic data by visual generative models. However, such models
usually do not meet the requirements of interactive rendering: inference is typically too slow, outputs
may vary from run to run, and generated content and structure may not fully align with the input
CG frame. DLSS 5 brings this learned knowledge to interactive rendering through a model designed
for real-time performance, deterministic inference, and strict alignment with the rendered scene.

1.2. What DLSS 5 is

DLSS 5 is a generative rendering stage — an approach we call 3D-guided neural rendering. It runs
after the renderer, inside the frame budget, and synthesizes the ﬁnal displayed image from the
rendered frame, for rasterized, path-traced, and hybrid pipelines alike. The model is a compact one-
step pixel-space diﬀusion model learned from real-world prior (Sec. 2). It changes how materials and
illumination look while staying conditioned on the frame the renderer produced (see Fig. 1). And
because it is conditioned on the rendered frame, better rendering upstream directly improves its
output: DLSS 5 composes with rasterization, ray tracing, and path tracing rather than competing
with them.
The rendered frame is the primary conditioning signal: dense and pixel-aligned, it pins down what
is in the scene, while artistic direction determines how far appearance may move. Neither half is
enough on its own. An oﬄine generative model can produce a beautiful image of the wrong scene;
a pass-through preserves the scene without improving it. DLSS 5 targets perceived realism, scene
ﬁdelity, and artistic direction at the same time — and it deliberately targets realism, not restyling
(Sec. 5).
The design is speciﬁed by four requirements:
   • Artistic intent preservation. The output preserves what the artists authored: geometric
     structure, object and character identity, material identity, composition, and the organization of
     light and shadow. Nothing enforces this at runtime — the supervision that encourages it acts
     during training, and every generated change carries some risk of drifting from the authored
     scene. Section 2.1 describes the supervision, and Section 4 measures the preservation.
   • Streaming. Inference is causal: one displayed output per rendered input, with no dependence
     on future frames. Oﬄine video generators usually buy temporal coherence by processing
     windows of frames together; an interactive renderer cannot wait for frames that have not
     happened yet (Sec. 2.5).
   • Budget. One denoising evaluation per rendered frame, within the compute and memory
     the application can spare. Sections 2–2.6 describe how conditioning, learning photo-realistic
     priors, one-step inference, and a hardware-oriented design close the gap.
   • Artistic direction. Appearance-model selection, strength values, chroma adjustment, and
     spatial and semantic masking decide which transformation is applied, how strongly, and where;
     given the same rendered sequence and settings, the output sequence is unchanged (Sec. 3).
The ﬁrst three requirements decide whether generation can run inside an interactive rendering loop
at all; artistic direction decides whether the result is usable in an authored title.

This subsection gives a simpliﬁed, conceptual account of the generation problem. For clarity it
considers a single frame in isolation and omits the temporal and artistic-direction conditioning of

A conditional generative image model represents a distribution pθ (x | c) over images x given a
conditioning signal c, such as a text prompt. Training shapes pθ so that samples are plausible
natural images consistent with c; inference draws from this distribution, and repeated draws are
expected to diﬀer.
DLSS 5 is asked to do something narrower. Its conditioning signal is the rendered frame r, and the
output should not be an arbitrary plausible image consistent with r: it should be a photographic
rendition of exactly the scene that r depicts. Let pphoto denote a distribution of photographic
appearance, learned statistically from photographic data, and let C(r) denote the set of images that
preserve the renderer-authored attributes of r: geometric structure, object and character identity,
material identity, composition, and light-and-shadow organization. DLSS 5 evaluates a deterministic
map x = fθ (r), trained so that, informally,

are compatible with one rendered frame — is resolved by the artistic direction of Section 3 rather
than by stochastic sampling.


2. System Overview
DLSS 5 is a renderer-conditioned streaming video model that processes each frame causally as
it arrives. This section separates ﬁve aspects of the system: grounded conditioning (Sec. 2.1),
construction of the task-speciﬁc student (Sec. 2.2 and 2.3), its pixel-space formulation (Sec. 2.4),
causal temporal-state streaming (Sec. 2.5), and hardware-oriented network design (Sec. 2.6).

2.1. Grounded Conditioning

The inference interface uses the engine-rendered RGB image as a dense, registered observation of
visible scene appearance. It provides dense, pixel-aligned evidence for object support, occlusion
boundaries, composition, and local material properties; engine motion vectors separately provide
temporal correspondence. Existing image generative models commonly rely on text embeddings,
exemplar images, or spatial control ﬁelds such as depth, edges, segmentation, and pose (Esser
et al., 2024; Imagen 3 Team, 2024; Black Forest Labs et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Li et al.,
2024). These conditions are eﬀective for general-purpose generation and editing, but they do not


                                                                                                  4
                                  DLSS 5: Real-Time Generative Rendering



uniquely determine the object identities, materials, visibility relationships, lighting decisions, and
pixel-aligned detail contained in an engine-rendered frame. DLSS 5 is therefore conditioned on the
rendered frame itself.
Native image-editing models can also modify images in a spatial and dense manner (Black Forest
Labs et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). However, their per-pixel ﬁdelity is not yet suﬃcient for pro-
duction rendering: small objects, thin structures, distant characters, and embedded text may be
altered, displaced, or removed during editing. Some of these errors arise because model capacity
is distributed across a broad range of tasks; in latent-space systems, additional error can come
from the lossy autoencoder round trip through which the edited image is re-synthesized (Sec. 2.4).
Interactive rendering allows little tolerance for such changes: the displayed stream must preserve
authored content in every frame.
Conditioning on rendered RGB alone, however, provides a very strict constraint on the output that
introduces challenges in achieving high realism. RGB entangles scene content with the appearance
already produced by the renderer. If the model follows the input too literally, it tends to reproduce
the same edges, colors, textures, and baked shading, leaving too little freedom to improve the ﬁnal
appearance. If the model is given too much freedom, it can drift from the authored content.
We resolve this challenge during training rather than at the runtime interface. During training, we
use renderer-derived albedo, surface normal, and lighting information to provide attribute-speciﬁc
consistency supervision. Albedo provides illumination-independent chromatic information, surface
normal provides local orientation information, and the lighting-derived signals supervise the au-
thored illumination structure. These signals are used only during training and are not inputs to the
shipped model. Artistic intent that is not already expressed in the rendered frame enters through
the artistic-direction control of Section 3.
These supervision targets constrain selected renderer-authored scene attributes, but they do not
deﬁne a target photographic appearance. Photographic appearance is instead learned statistically
from photographic data, while renderer-derived supervision discourages changes that would conﬂict
with the authored scene. Within this supervision envelope, the model is free to change what the
authored attributes do not pin down: ﬁne surface shading, the way materials appear under light,
and the overall tone of ambient and indirect illumination.
The grounding is essential for preserving object identity and maintaining semantic alignment with
the rendered frame. For example, albedo consistency helps preserve the intrinsic chroma of an object
while allowing the model to synthesize more realistic local illumination. The lighting supervision
anchors the direct-lighting structure and helps preserve the intended placement and orientation of
shadows.

2.2. Learning Photo-realistic Priors

Foundational generative models trained at scale on photographic data encode a rich prior over
real-world appearance, including the statistical relationships among materials, illumination, surface
geometry, and scene context as they appear in photographs. These learned appearance statistics
capture eﬀects that would be prohibitively expensive to reproduce through ﬁrst-principles simula-
tion at production scale (Sec. 5.2). However, such models are unsuitable as real-time rendering
components. Because they are usually designed to accept diverse forms of conditioning and synthe-
size open-ended content from scratch, they require substantial model capacity and typically rely on
iterative inference, placing them far outside an interactive frame budget.
Real-time rendering enhancement is a substantially narrower problem. The rendered frame already

speciﬁes the scene content, geometry, composition, and motion; the model only needs to learn how to
enhance its appearance. DLSS 5 therefore uses a dedicated network specialized for this task, focusing
its capacity on the real-world appearance priors relevant to rendered frames. This specialization
trades open-ended generative capability for eﬃciency: the resulting model is substantially smaller
and faster, while retaining rich knowledge of real-world materials, lighting, and appearance.
Figure 1 illustrates this behavior on representative rendered frames. It shows the rendered input
and the corresponding DLSS 5 output, together with enlarged crops highlighting the most visible
changes. The enhanced outputs remain aligned with the rendered content while exhibiting richer
material and illumination appearance consistent with the learned prior. Systematic comparisons
and content preservation measurements are presented in Sec. 4.

2.3. One Step Diﬀusion

DLSS 5 employs a single-step diﬀusion inference rather than iterative stochastic sampling. Each
enhanced frame is produced in a single deterministic inference step; given the same rendered se-
quence and conﬁguration, the system produces the same output sequence. Although additional
sampling steps can improve distribution modeling in general-purpose diﬀusion models, they do not
necessarily yield substantially better perceptual quality for this strongly conditioned enhancement
task. As demonstrated in Sec. 4, DLSS 5 achieves high perceptual quality within a real-time frame
budget.

2.4. Pixel Space Diﬀusion

Most large diﬀusion models operate in the latent space of a variational autoencoder (VAE): images
are encoded into a compact latent representation, denoised there, and decoded back to pixels (Rom-
bach et al., 2022). This reduces computation, but the encode–decode round trip is lossy. Fine
structure is re-synthesized by the decoder rather than carried through, and small latent-space de-
viations surface as shifted edges, altered textures, and re-drawn detail. A VAE is therefore not
accurate enough to guarantee the art, narrative, and semantic preservation that a production-grade
real-time generator requires: encoding and decoding introduce drift in exactly the attributes DLSS 5
must preserve.
DLSS 5 instead performs diﬀusion directly in pixel space. The choice is dictated by the preservation
requirements above rather than by precedent; pixel-space generation at scale has likewise been
favored in recent work (Hoogeboom et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2026). No learned autoencoder appears
in the inference path: the model consumes rendered RGB and produces displayed RGB, and the
network’s internal feature maps (Sec. 2.6) serve as intermediate computation rather than as a lossy
bottleneck through which the image must be reconstructed.
Generating in the same space in which the renderer produced the image is what allows DLSS 5 to
leave the renderer’s structure untouched. In practice, DLSS 5 does not change geometry or structural
edges, and its preservation extends to the ﬁnest level of the input signal: even the individual pixel
aliasing of the rendered input survives generation.

Chunk-based architectures are widely used to generate temporally coherent videos (Wan et al., 2025;
Hunyuan, 2025; NVIDIA, 2026). These architectures process multiple frames jointly, typically using
temporal attention to exchange information within a ﬁnite chunk. Although this strategy is eﬀective
for improving temporal stability, it is poorly suited to high-resolution real-time interactive rendering
for two primary reasons. First, multiple frames must be buﬀered and jointly processed before the
corresponding outputs become available. As illustrated in Figure. 2, this buﬀering introduces ad-
ditional latency between a user action and the corresponding displayed response, thereby reducing
interactivity. Second, the computational and memory costs of joint spatiotemporal processing in-
crease with the number of frames in each chunk. Under a ﬁxed hardware budget, distributing these
resources across multiple frames constrains the budget available to each frame and can therefore
limit the maximum spatial resolution.
In contrast, DLSS 5 adopts a causal frame-streaming formulation that performs immediate inference
once the current renderer RGB frame is ready, and it requires no future frames. Consequently, each
enhanced frame becomes available shortly after its corresponding renderer frame is processed, while
the peak inference memory remains small and constant with respect to sequence duration. Rendered
RGB frames anchor the corresponding predictions to the current scene contents, preserving semantic
ﬁdelity, while the streaming model maintains temporal coherence across successive enhanced frames.
This formulation enables low-latency, high-resolution enhancement, including at 4K, and is therefore
well aligned with the requirements of real-time gaming.

3.1. Selecting an Appearance

One useful framework we ﬁnd is to consider that DLSS 5 is a transformation from a distribution over
computer generated frames to a target distribution (see Fig. 4). In practice, even the distribution
over “photographs” varies wildly and encompasses many choices like ﬁlm, digital, lens, color grading,
and tonemapping. Ideally, DLSS 5 would be both large enough and fast enough to provide enough
control to explore the entirety of plausible photographic mappings in a single model, but this
remains an open and diﬃcult problem. As an approximation, the current system samples this
family with a small set of curated models whose learned output distributions diﬀer in tone, color,
and material appearance, such as in their treatment of vegetation, light, and skin to name a few, as
shown in Figure 5. Together, these models represent a discretized sampled subset of the available
photographic-appearance mappings.
As 3D-guided neural rendering matures and evolves, we expect more and more control over the
diversity of target styles to emerge, allowing developers and artists to ﬁnd ideal mappings that
align with their game’s desired genre, tone, contrast, lighting and material response.

3.2. Separating Tone and Structure

In addition to output style, artists need to determine where and how much enhancement is applied.
We observed that the overall enhancements produced by DLSS 5 can be intuitively categorized as (1)
changes in ﬁne detail of surfaces and adjustments to local contrast, (2) shifts in broad luminance and
illumination of scene elements. To enable independent adjustment of each category of enhancement,
DLSS 5 exposes structure and tone strength modulation parameters respectively. At zero strength,
the system is conﬁgured to return an enhancement-free input; at unit strength, it applies the full
learned transformation. As implemented, the structure and tone controls behaviorally correspond
to high and low spatial frequency bands of the image content.
Fig. 6 illustrates the eﬀects of the two strength coeﬃcients on the target output.
The structure strength, tone strength, and model subset parameters are incorporated as learned
conditioning inputs. This simpliﬁes the deployed algorithm, and fuses an expensive multi-frequency
decomposition operation into the network which minimizes the resulting run time at inference.

characters and other hero assets. DLSS 5 therefore determines where enhancement applies through

4. Evaluation
We evaluate DLSS 5’s results based on three complementary aspects: perceived image quality
and photographic realism, content and identity alignment with the renderer-authored input, and
structure alignment with the rendered scene. We use the same evaluation content across methods
wherever possible so that these properties can be interpreted jointly. At the end of this section, we
also report the speed and VRAM of DLSS 5.

4.1. Setup

Evaluation data. Our quantitative evaluation contains 109 aligned scenes. The set includes
both path-traced (PT) and non-path-traced (non-PT) rendered inputs and covers characters, envi-
ronments, complex materials, and varied lighting. The main comparison reports results over the full
set. We additionally report a PT/non-PT split as a robustness check in Sec. 4.3.2; the two subsets
contain diﬀerent scenes and therefore should not be interpreted as a controlled causal comparison
of rendering quality.

detailed in Sec. 4.2. We also include a matched-budget renderer-scaling reference — the same scenes
rendered with 4× more rays within the same total frame time — used for qualitative comparison
(Fig. 10).

4.2. Metrics

Image quality / realism. We measure image quality as perceived photographic realism using a
blinded pairwise user study. All model outputs are generated at 3840 × 2160. For the user study,
participants are shown two results from diﬀerent models and are required to select the result that
appears more photorealistic and less CG-rendered, considering lighting, shadows, reﬂections, and
visible artifacts. They are explicitly instructed not to penalize content diﬀerences between the two
outputs. The user study therefore measures image quality and realism only, rather than preservation
of the input content or structure.

Content and identity alignment. We use DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2023) and LPIPS (Zhang
et al., 2018) to measure how closely the generated RGB output preserves the visible content and
identity of the CG input. Both the CG inputs and generated outputs are at 3840 × 2160. DINOv2
patch distance is computed after resizing both images to 672 × 378 and compares spatially corre-
sponding normalized ViT-B/14 patch features using cosine distance; lower is better. Image LPIPS
is computed directly between the full-resolution 3840 × 2160 CG input and generated output using
AlexNet LPIPS, without spatial resizing; lower is better. These metrics are used strictly as content
and identity preservation metrics and are not interpreted as measures of photographic quality.

Structure alignment. To evaluate whether the generated image preserves the underlying scene
structure, we compare the albedo, surface normals, and depth of the generated image with those of
its corresponding CG input, all at 3840×2160. We trained a single estimator to extract these buﬀers
from both the input and the output. These comparisons measure whether the scene attributes of
the generated output remain consistent with those of the CG frame.
For albedo, we report SSIM (Wang et al., 2004) and PSNR at the native 3840 × 2160 resolution,
together with LPIPS computed after resizing the predicted albedo maps to 960 × 540. For surface
normals, we report mean angular error, accuracy within 11.25◦ , and edge F1, all at 3840 × 2160; the
angular metrics measure local surface-orientation consistency, while edge F1 measures preservation
of geometric boundaries. For depth, we report Spearman rank correlation, SSIM, edge F1, and edge
Chamfer distance at 3840 × 2160, capturing relative depth ordering, spatial depth structure, and
depth discontinuities. Metric directions are indicated in Table 1.

5. Discussion and Limitations

5.1. Perceived Realism and Physically Based Ground Truth

A natural question is how the results in this report relate to physically based ground truth. For
a ﬁxed scene representation and light-transport model, the renderer-deﬁned physical reference is
the result approached as sampling converges. DLSS 5 does not attempt to reconstruct or estimate
this reference in the way a conventional reconstruction method would. Instead, it produces a condi-
tional output using statistical appearance priors learned from photographic data, while remaining
constrained by the rendered input and by renderer-based supervision during training.
Our evaluation includes a matched-budget renderer baseline that spends the same frame time on
4× more rays (see section 4.3.1). When DLSS 5’s output is judged more realistic than rendered
output, that judgment should be interpreted only as evidence about perceived photographic realism.
It does not imply greater ﬁdelity to the renderer-deﬁned physical reference. Such perceptual judg-
ments can be inﬂuenced by statistical properties learned from photographs, including characteristic
appearances of skin, hair, materials, and local shading. DLSS 5 may improve these appearance
cues when they are weak in the rendered input, even when doing so does not make the result more
physically accurate.
The renderer remains the authoritative source of scene content and the reference for physical cor-
rectness. DLSS 5 operates on displayed appearance rather than redeﬁning that reference. Artistic
direction determines the selected appearance transformation, including its strength or whether it is
applied at all.
This distinction is both an interpretation of our results and a limitation of the method: DLSS 5
neither estimates nor explicitly targets the renderer-deﬁned physical reference. Section 5.2 discusses
why this formulation is useful in practice. The following subsections describe two additional struc-
tural limitations: the restriction to photorealistic rendering and the dependence on input quality.

5.2. The Practical Gap to First-Principles Photorealism

Photorealism is not fundamentally limited by a lack of physical understanding. With suﬃciently
accurate light transport, assets captured at real-world ﬁdelity, and fully physically based material
models, rendering can already produce imagery that appears photographic. However, in practice,
production renderers can only approximate all three because of limits in computation, memory, and
asset complexity.
Some eﬀects remain diﬃcult to simulate faithfully even with substantial compute. In skin, light
enters the surface, scatters through layered tissue and ﬁne structure, and re-emerges elsewhere;
simulating this transport faithfully is beyond even generous oﬄine budgets. Other materials are
physically well understood but remain expensive to represent and evaluate under real-time compute
and memory — semi-translucent media, silk, hair, fur, snow, and their interactions. More commonly,
the limitation arises earlier in the pipeline: to meet the budgets of shipping platforms, developers

                                 DLSS 5: Real-Time Generative Rendering



reduce texture resolution, geometric complexity, and material detail. As a result, even eﬀects that
are straightforward to model may be represented at lower ﬁdelity than the rendering system could
ideally support.
The practical gap to photorealism is therefore often a problem of scale (i.e., computation, mem-
ory, and asset production) rather than one of missing physical principles. DLSS 5 introduces a
complementary approach. Instead of explicitly pushing every approximation closer to its physical
reference, it uses statistical properties learned from photographic data to restore appearance cues
that may be missing or weakened by those approximations, particularly in material response and
illumination. DLSS 5 does not reproduce the omitted physical simulation, nor does it modify the
authored scene. Rather, it is trained to enrich perceived material and lighting appearance while
remaining conditioned on the rendered frame (Sec. 2.1) and subject to artistic control (Sec. 3).

5.3. Limitation to Photorealistic Rendering

The current objective of DLSS 5 is to produce vivid, photorealistic game imagery by generating
realistic lighting, materials, and ﬁne texture details. Therefore, the system is not designed for
games whose visual identity depends on a strongly stylized aesthetic. For example, voxel-based,
illustrated, and cartoon-like games intentionally use non-photorealistic visual languages. Applying
the current DLSS 5 to such content may work against the art direction rather than improve it.
A more general system would allow developers to specify the desired rendering style and trans-
form a game scene accordingly while preserving its geometry, semantics, and temporal consistency.
Supporting such arbitrary stylistic direction is an important avenue for future work. The current
version of DLSS 5, however, remains limited to photorealistic rendering.

5.4. Input Image Quality Constraint

DLSS 5 produces an RGB image and does not modify the engine’s underlying geometry, scene state,
or G-buﬀer contents. Its output is therefore fundamentally constrained by the information available
in the rendered input.
Although DLSS 5 can enrich local appearance, it cannot reliably recover scene information that is
absent, incorrect, or severely degraded in the input. Missing, aliased, or otherwise corrupted scene
evidence may be preserved or ampliﬁed. In such cases, the rendered observation does not provide
enough evidence to support a faithful enhancement for DLSS 5.
Low-quality scene rendering is particularly challenging: if object boundaries or structural details
are weakly represented in the input, DLSS 5 cannot be expected to reconstruct their intended form
reliably. The model is therefore better understood as an appearance-enhancement model rather
than a mechanism for correcting arbitrary upstream rendering errors. It can improve the perceived
realism of a valid and suﬃciently informative input, but it cannot guarantee the reconstruction of
missing geometry, object identity, or authored details. Figure 11 illustrates an example in which
the aliasing and degraded detail in the window frames limit the quality of the corresponding region
in the DLSS 5 output.
This behavior reﬂects a deliberate trade-oﬀ. Where the input evidence is defective, a generative
model could invent a plausible replacement, but an invented replacement risks changing what the
image depicts. DLSS 5 is trained to take the safer side of that trade: it preserves the geometric
structure of the input exactly — including defects such as aliasing (Sec. 2.4) — rather than risk
altering the story or meaning of the image. A visible defect can be ﬁxed by improving the input; a
changed meaning cannot be ﬁxed downstream at all.

This dependence on the rendered input also makes the model sensitive to distribution shifts be-

conﬁgurations remains an important direction for future work.


6. Conclusion
DLSS 5 extends the DLSS family beyond pixel reconstruction to renderer-conditioned generative
enhancement. It learns photographic appearance priors from data with a compact, one-step pixel-
space streaming diﬀusion model. Renderer-derived consistency supervision during training encour-
ages alignment with authored scene attributes, while causal frame-wise inference supports low la-
tency and temporal stability at high resolution. The controls govern appearance style, enhancement
strength, color response, and spatial and semantic masking; together with deterministic inference,
they limit unintended modiﬁcations to authored content and make the selected enhancement behav-
ior reproducible.
Our evaluation shows that DLSS 5 can operate within the target frame and memory budgets while
producing a more photorealistic appearance, including richer material details and more expressive
lighting. At the same time, it preserves scene structure and renderer-authored content, and main-
tains temporal stability. Because it operates downstream of the conventional renderer, DLSS 5
integrates learned generative enhancement into existing rendering workﬂows without assuming re-
sponsibility other functions including scene simulation and animation.
More broadly, integrating real-time generative models with conventional rendering pipelines can
expand the expressive capacity of interactive graphics, and DLSS 5 ﬁrstly demonstrate the possibilies
in this direction. This increased capacity introduces a corresponding need for greater predictability,
directability, and ﬁdelity to developers’ intent. Future work will therefore pursue improvements
in visual ﬁdelity, generalization, eﬃciency, and temporal robustness, together with ﬁner-grained
spatial, semantic, stylistic, and temporal controls. These directions point toward a new paradigm
for real-time graphics in which learned generative enhancement augments, rather than replaces,
conventional rendering.
