                                         1       I NTRODUCTION

                                         A force applied anywhere on a robot’s body travels through its links to the joints, which then move
                                         differently from what their commands alone would produce. The encoders record that difference, so
                                         the joints of every arm and leg double as contact sensors. Legged robots exploit this proprioceptive
                                         signal to walk, run, and climb stairs blind (Lee et al., 2020; Siekmann et al., 2021a;b; Kumar et al.,
                                         2021), and humanoids to walk and track whole-body motions (Radosavovic et al., 2024; Cheng et al.,
                                         2024; He et al., 2024). In these settings, however, contacts are large and forceful, and joints often
                                         comply meaningfully while recording measurable deviations. Can robots exploit the same signal for
                                         precise, contact-rich assembly?
                                         Precise assembly with a robotic arm occupies the opposite regime: the robot must find an opening
                                         with a millimeter or less of clearance, bring gear teeth into mesh, or catch a thread, through light
                                         contact whose effect on the joints is easy to miss. We study this question by training blind assembly
                                         policies that have no information outside of their own joint sensing, and verify that proprioception
                                         contains enough information for three such tasks: peg insertion, gear meshing, and nut threading
                                         (figure 2). In the real world, our peg insertion policy inserts eight pegs of different cross-sections,
                                         three of them outside its training distribution, with 0.5–1.5 mm of clearance per side, in 73% of
                                         237 trials, without being given the hole’s location, the peg’s pose in its grasp, the part’s size, or its
                                         clearance (section 3.4). To our knowledge, this is the first such demonstration.
                                         We train our policies entirely in simulation with reinforcement learning and deploy the peg insertion
                                         policy in the real world without fine-tuning (figure 1). Training randomizes part geometry, contact

On all three tasks, probing the blind policies’ learned representations and comparing them with
matched visual policies revealed two interesting properties of visually blind behaviors:
 • Visually blind yet intelligent. The learned behavior is not an open-loop pattern: its learned repre-
   sentation encoding its own proprioceptive inputs contains meaningful task-relevant information
   that the policy itself heavily relies on to adjust its behavior (section 3.2).
 • Proprioception generalizes better. Because a blind policy depends on the part only through its
   contacts, it degrades far less than vision on part shapes and sizes it has not seen during training
   (section 3.3).
Training for this robustness requires diverse parts: thousands of different assemblies, generated
procedurally and simulated in parallel, each with its millimeter clearance intact. In MuJoCo (Todorov
et al., 2012), building holes from convex primitives by hand does not scale to random shapes, and
the automatic alternatives, convex decomposition and signed-distance-field (SDF) collision, are less
accurate and slower at these clearances. We therefore built mjfit, which procedurally generates
pegs and holes from any planar outline, as well as threads and gears, out of exact convex prisms. To
summarize, our contributions are:
1. Blind policies, trained purely in simulation, that learn peg insertion, gear meshing, and nut
   threading from proprioception alone, given no fixture pose, grasp, or part model (section 3.1),
   with analyses showing that on all three tasks they search reactively, acting on the contact state their
   joint histories encode rather than following a fixed pattern (section 3.2), and that they generalize
   to unseen part shapes and sizes better than matched visual policies (section 3.3);
2. The first sim-to-real policy for precise insertion from proprioception alone: trained only in
   simulation, the peg insertion policy transfers zero-shot to a real robot and inserts pegs of different
   cross-sections, including pegs outside its training distribution (section 3.4);
3. mjfit, an open-source procedural generator of assemblies for MuJoCo (pegs and holes, threads,
   and gears) with exact convex collision geometry, which supplies the thousands of different training
   assemblies, and a benchmark against analytic ground truth in which it is more accurate and faster
   than convex decomposition and MuJoCo’s SDF collision (section 2.2).

2.2     S IMULATING TIGHT INSERTION AT SCALE

Training on this distribution means simulating thousands of parallel worlds that each hold a different,
randomly generated assembly, with per-side clearances down to 0.5 mm. MuJoCo collides meshes
through their convex hulls, so a concave hole must be given an SDF or built from convex pieces,
which simulation benchmarks assemble from boxes by hand (Zhu et al., 2020; Tao et al., 2024; Zakka
et al., 2025).

Exact convex decomposition. mjfit automates the hand-built hole and generalizes it to arbitrary
outlines. Given the planar outline of a hole, optionally with islands such as connector pins, it emits a
ready-to-simulate MJCF model in which every contact surface is a MuJoCo primitive or a convex
prism (figure 4). The plate around the hole is split into convex polygons and extruded; the peg is the
hole outline offset inward by the clearance, so every peg edge sits exactly the clearance from its hole
edge, and islands become pockets in a hollow peg. Curves are tessellated by a circumscribed polygon,
so the wall never intrudes into the true hole, as it does in rings of boxes that inscribe the circle (Zhu
et al., 2020); the only error is extra play at the corners, c (1/ cos(π/n) − 1) for a circle of n sides at
radial clearance c. Because the construction knows which region must stay empty, the user specifies
the clearance directly rather than tuning a concavity tolerance and checking the result. Companion
generators apply the same idea to bolt–nut pairs and spur gears, and a sampler draws random outlines,
islands, and dimensions, so one training run can hold thousands of different assemblies.

2.3                           P OLICY AND TRAINING

Policy. At each step, the actor (figure 7) observes its joint positions and velocities, the gripper pose
and twist computed from them by forward kinematics, and its previous action. It never receives the
hole pose, the part geometry, a wrench estimate, or an image. A temporal convolutional encoder
compresses the last 0.6 s of observations into a latent, and an MLP maps the latent and the current
observation to joint-position increments. A joint impedance controller tracks them on an arm model
whose armature, damping, and friction were identified from real-world measurements. Additional
training details in Appendix A.

Training. We use PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) with a sparse reward: a bonus on success and one-
time bonuses for intermediate milestones, such as the peg’s first capture by the opening (Appendix A.1
gives the gear’s and nut’s). Two training-only signals speed up learning from the sparse reward
without giving the actor privileged information: an asymmetric critic (Pinto et al., 2018) that also
sees the part’s pose relative to the fixture and the true contact labels, and the contact supervision
described next. Randomizing dynamics and geometry (Tobin et al., 2017; Peng et al., 2018) covers
the ranges of Table 1.

Contact supervision. A blind policy has to infer contact from how the arm moves. To give that
inference a dense training signal, we supervise the actor’s latent with the contact state it cannot
observe. At every step, the simulator labels points sampled on the mating features of the part and
fixture, as free, touching but misaligned, or aligned (figure 7, top; Appendix A details each task’s
labels). A small contact decoder predicts these labels from the latent zt , and its class-weighted
cross-entropy is added to the PPO loss (Jaderberg et al., 2017). The gradient of this loss trains the
encoder, but the prediction never enters the policy, so nothing privileged reaches the actor and the
decoder is dropped at deployment.


Visual policies. To isolate the sensing modality, we train visual policies with the same task, resets,
randomization, reward, actions, and success check. A camera rigidly mounted on the wrist renders
a 64×64 RGB image of the held part and the fixture. The visual policy sees only images, over the
same 0.6 s history, through a compact CNN.


3             R ESULTS

We design experiments to answer the following questions:
              1. (section 3.1) Can a policy that senses only its own joints learn precise assembly tasks including
                 peg insertion, gear meshing, and nut threading?
              2. (section 3.2) Do blind policies learn intelligent, contact-reactive search, or is it learning
                 scripted patterns?
              3. (section 3.3) Do blind policies generalize better than visual policies to part geometries outside
                 the training distribution?
              4. (section 3.4) Can blind policies that rely on contact-rich search be transferred zero shot with
                 high fidelity to the real world?


3.1            P ROPRIOCEPTION IS SUFFICIENT

All three tasks achieve near-perfect success on in-distribution geometries (figure 11, left). The joint
history alone therefore carries enough information about contact to find an opening, bring teeth into
mesh, and engage a thread.
Compared with visual policies, which can typically observe the poses of the grasped and mating parts
directly, blind policies may be slower. On peg insertion, the blind policy has to search for the opening
through contact, so it takes a less direct path and seats later (figure 9a, b) than a visual policy. The
gap is smaller on the other two tasks (Appendix C), plausibly because the grasped part occludes more
of the mating part from the camera’s view, so the visual policy, too, has to search to some extent.

3.2                 V ISUALLY BLIND BUT INTELLIGENT

If blind policies search reactively rather than follow a script, their history latent zt , through which
everything the actor knows about the past passes (figure 7), must encode the state of contact. To test
this, we roll out each policy in simulation and label every state with one of seven modes of its task,
computed from the privileged part pose. For the peg, these are lifted, beside the plate, searching
  far from the opening or near it, partly engaged, and fully inside, either moving or stuck; the
gear’s and nut’s modes follow similar stages of their tasks (Appendix C). We then fit a linear probe
that predicts the mode from zt .
On all three tasks, the probe separates modes that call for different actions, such as lifted, engaged,
and stuck (figure 10), and does so more accurately from zt than from the current observation alone
(accuracy 61–73%, against 41–64%; chance 14%). Furthermore, the policy heavily relies on these
inferred contact states to choose actions. Swapping in the latent of a state in another mode changes
the action 1.3–3.0× as much as a swap within the same mode (figure 15; Appendix C). Fitted only in
simulation, the peg probe decodes the same sequence of modes on the real robot (figure 10a).
Reacting to contacts also makes a blind assembly efficient. A fixed pattern sweeps the same path
regardless of what the arm feels, whereas a reactive policy can use the information gathered through
contact, such as the peg catching the rim of the opening, to steer its search. We test this on peg
insertion, where a scripted blind baseline is standard: spiral search, which moves the peg outward
along a spiral until it drops into the hole, is a common strategy for insertion under position uncertainty
in industrial assembly (Chhatpar & Branicky, 2001; Park et al., 2017; Marvel et al., 2018). From the
same starts, the blind policy travels a median 37 mm before seating, against 95 mm for an optimistic
spiral that starts flush with the hole opening, and is given the peg’s orientation and the best starting
direction (figure 9c).

3.4      Z ERO - SHOT TRANSFER TO THE REAL WORLD

Setup. We deploy the frozen blind policy on a Flexiv Rizon 4S with a Robotiq 2F-85 at 50 Hz, with
no real-world data or fine-tuning. It receives only encoder-derived joint and gripper state; no camera,
force/torque sensor, or hole pose enters the policy. The deployed policy is a policy trained on random
convex, smooth, and mixed cross-sections under a handle (Table 2). We test eight 3D-printed pegs
(figure 12): five in-distribution objects, and three that are drawn from outside the training distribution,
a tighter clearance (0.5 mm per side), a larger cross-section, and a taller handle. Each trial starts
from a start state drawn from the simulated reset distribution, with the peg grasped at a randomized
position and tilt and touching the fixture near the hole. An operator marks a trial as successful once
the peg is seated.

Results. One policy, with no parameter changes, succeeds in 173 of 237 trials (73%; figure 12),
pressing onto the surface, sweeping, and dropping in once the opening is under the peg, as in
simulation (figure 1). It succeeds in 73–80% of trials on each in-distribution peg, and in 66%, 66%,
and 72% on the tighter, larger, and taller-handled pegs, respectively.

4     R ELATED WORK

Robotic insertion. Classical peg-in-hole assembly combines compliance with search patterns such
as spirals, rasters, and tilting (Whitney, 1982; Chhatpar & Branicky, 2001; Marvel et al., 2018), in
some cases without force feedback (Park et al., 2017); these strategies assume a known grasp and a
hole within the pattern and are tuned for each part. Learned insertion policies usually add force/torque
sensing (Inoue et al., 2017; Beltran-Hernandez et al., 2020), wrist vision (Schoettler et al., 2020;
Bogunowicz et al., 2020; Spector & Di Castro, 2021; Haugaard et al., 2021; Seo et al., 2025), or both
(Lee et al., 2019; Luo et al., 2021; Watson et al., 2020). Wrist force/torque sensing combined with
a soft wrist has allowed policies to tolerate 10 mm of hole-position error and an unseen peg shape
(Fuchioka et al., 2024), while a blind dexterous policy relying on fingertip tactile sensing (Pan et al.)
was demonstrated for precise insertion. Others (Tang et al., 2023; 2024; Noseworthy et al., 2025;
Narang et al., 2022) observe joint state and a direct goal pose from perception or calibration with a
few millimeters of error. Our policy instead uses proprioception alone: it is given no hole pose, part
model, or force signal, and must locate the hole through contact while the peg’s pose in its grasp is
unknown.

Proprioceptive policies. Legged robots traverse rough terrain with policies built on histories of
onboard state (Hwangbo et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2020; Siekmann et al., 2021b; Kumar et al., 2021;
Radosavovic et al., 2024). In manipulation, proprioceptive policies reorient unseen objects in hand
(Chen et al., 2021; Qi et al., 2022), and Blind Dexterity treats joint and action histories as a whole-body
tactile channel (Bhatt et al., 2026). These tasks involve forceful contact or centimeter tolerances;


                                                                    9
Preprint




insertion needs neither. In imitation learning, robot state can hurt (Team et al., 2024) by suppressing
visual learning (Lu et al., 2026).

Simulating tight contact. Approximate convex decomposition (Mamou & Ghorbel, 2009; Wei
et al., 2022) tolerates concavity relative to the mesh size, which can exceed an assembly’s clearance.
Exact polygon partitions are classical (Keil, 1985), and simulation benchmarks build holes from
primitives by hand (Zhu et al., 2020; Tao et al., 2024; Zakka et al., 2025); mjfit automates this for
arbitrary outlines and generates assemblies in bulk. SDFs handle concavity directly and underlie
GPU assembly simulation in Factory and its successors (Narang et al., 2022; Macklin et al., 2020;
Tang et al., 2023), whose assets obtain their clearance approximately, by displacing mesh vertices or
eroding voxels (Tang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025); SDF accuracy depends on field resolution and
contact search. Newton combines SDFs with hydroelastic contact (The Newton developers, 2025),
and barrier methods simulate threads and gear trains without interpenetration (Ferguson et al., 2021;
Lan et al., 2022). Our benchmark (section 2.2) follows the simulator comparisons of Erez et al. (2015)
and Le Lidec et al. (2024). CRISP (Lee et al., 2026) recently compared engines on tight insertion
and threading; we instead keep MuJoCo’s solver fixed and vary only the collision geometry, using
passive probes with analytic answers and speed–accuracy Pareto fronts.

5   L IMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION
We trained blind policies that insert pegs, mesh gears, and thread nuts from proprioception alone, with
no fixture pose, grasp, or part model, on thousands of assemblies that mjfit generates with exact
collision geometry. The peg insertion policy transfers zero-shot to the real world, and in simulation,
blind policies generalize to unseen parts far better than matched visual policies. The peg insertion
policy is, however, a last-mile manipulation skill, and its limits are those of one. First, it assumes
it starts in contact: every training episode begins with the peg already touching the fixture a few
centimeters from the hole, so another motion, scripted or visual, must bring it there. Second, its
behavior is local. It searches the surface around where it starts and corrects the misalignments it met
in training, but it cannot handle events that call for more than a local correction, such as a peg that
slides out from the grasp and lands far from the hole.

A     T RAINING DETAILS

Study and deployed policies. To cover as much real-world variation as possible, the deployed peg
policy (section 3.4) is trained on the full ranges of part geometry, physics parameters, and object pose
that we can (limited by physics stability, geometric constraints like the gripper width, etc.). We also
train similarly diverse gear and nut policies for the latent probe (section 3.2; Appendix A.2). In order
to test the policies’ capacity for generalizing out of the training distribution, we design study training
ranges for all three tasks (section 3) that are more narrow (Table 1), so that we can systematically
widen each property beyond training (section 3.3).

A.1    S TUDY POLICIES

Tasks. Physics runs at 1 kHz under a 50 Hz policy, and each world holds one part and fixture drawn
from mjfit’s generators. Peg insertion: each episode starts with the peg touching the fixture’s top
face, a median 1 cm from the hole center. It succeeds when the entire peg bottom face is seated in the
hole. Bonuses of +1 reward the peg’s first capture by the opening and success. Contact labels mark
each of 64 points on the peg’s bottom edge as free (more than 1 mm above the fixture’s top face),
in contact (on the rim or top face, within 1 mm), or inside the opening. An episode lasts at most 5 s.
Gear meshing: each world holds a spur-gear set from mjfit’s gear generator, and the robot holds
the gear by a printed boss. Each episode starts with the gear resting on the shaft tip, off the shaft axis,
with its teeth out of phase with the anchors’. It succeeds when the bore surrounds the shaft and the
gear sits within 1 mm of the shaft’s collar, which the anchors’ teeth allow only in mesh. Bonuses
reward the first capture of the shaft, the first mesh with every anchor, and success; contact labels
mark 64 points on the shaft’s rim (free, on the gear, or inside the bore) and 64 on the gear’s tooth
tips (free, on an anchor, or in mesh); an episode lasts at most 10 s. Nut threading: each world holds
an ISO metric nut and bolt pair from mjfit’s thread generator. Each episode starts with the nut
resting on the bolt tip, tilted and off the bolt axis, short of the turn at which the thread catches it. It
succeeds a quarter turn after the thread catches, with the nut within 1 mm of the bolt axis; bonuses
reward seating, capture, each new extent of the turn, and success; contact labels mark 64 points on
the rim of the nut’s bore (free, on the bolt’s tip, around the bolt, or caught by the thread, within a
band of 0.29 pitch, 1 mm at M30×3.5); an episode lasts at most 15 s. Every episode also ends when
the part moves far from the fixture, and a nut episode when the nut leaves the grasp.

Architecture. The actor observes 33 values per step: seven joint positions, seven joint velocities,
the gripper pose and twist (twelve), and its previous action (seven). The nut policy’s actor encodes
the gripper orientation by two columns of its rotation matrix rather than by Euler angles (36 values),
since the wrist turns through half a revolution while threading. A temporal convolutional encoder
maps the last 30 observations (0.6 s) to a 32-dimensional latent; an MLP (256, 256, 128 units) maps
the latent and the current observation to seven joint-position increments, scaled by the action scale
of Table 1 and added to the measured joint positions. The critic sees the part’s pose relative to the
fixture, the contact labels, and, for the nut, the thread’s geometry (240, 434, and 317 inputs for the
peg, gear, and nut).

PPO. 12,288 worlds, 32-step rollouts, 5 epochs of 20 minibatches (24 for the gear and nut), fixed
learning rate 3 × 10−4 , clip 0.2, γ = 0.98, λ = 0.95, entropy coefficient 0.003, value-loss coefficient
1 with clipping, gradient norm clipped at 1, and initial action standard deviation 0.5. The contact
decoder has two hidden layers of 128 units and class weights 0.25 (free), 3.0 (contact), and 2.0 (inside,
in mesh, around the bolt, or caught). The visual policies see 64×64 wrist RGB images over the same
30 steps.

widths of 0.44–0.48 of the pitch, face widths of 8–15 mm, 0.2–0.6 mm of backlash per anchor, and
0.5–1 mm of bore clearance, on 4–6 mm shafts 3–10 mm above the gear; with the boss they weigh
128–276 g, set by their geometry. Nuts are 870 ISO metric nut and bolt pairs, M24–M42 at coarse
and fine pitch, with 0.05–0.2 mm of flank clearance, across flats and heights within 10% of ISO 4032,
and steel masses of 73–855 g. Their contact and pose ranges are wider too: anchor-tooth friction
0.2–1.0, thread friction 0.2–0.4, and the deployed peg policy’s plate, finger, gripper, fixture, and
gripper-rotation ranges. Gears start a median 12 mm off the shaft axis; nuts start a median 19 mm
off the bolt axis and 60–170◦ of turning short of the thread catching. With deterministic actions, the
gear policy succeeds in 98% of 8,192 episodes on 4,096 unseen gear sets drawn from its training
distribution, and the nut policy in 93% of 8,192 episodes on 512 unseen nut and bolt pairs drawn
likewise.
